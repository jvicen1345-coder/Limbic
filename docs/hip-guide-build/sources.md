# Hip Examination Playbook — verified source bank

Every entry here was looked up in Europe PMC and read. The **Supports** column is what the
freely readable text actually says; the **Does not support** column is what a reader might
expect it to say and it does not. That second column is the point of this file: the shoulder
guide's first draft carried 37 errors in 545 claims, and the ones that survive re-reading are
the claims attached to a real paper that does not quite say them.

Access is recorded because the guide's rule is that an unmarked value is traced to something a
reader can reach for free. Where only the abstract is free, only what the abstract carries may
be quoted unmarked.

Status: **in progress.** Thirteen papers read; four values formally recorded as untraceable.
Nothing below has been drafted into content yet.

## Range of motion

### Roach KE, Miles TP. Normal hip and knee active range of motion: the relationship to age.
*Phys Ther* 1991;71(9):656-665 · PMID 1881956 · **abstract only**

- **Supports** — NHANES I national probability sample; goniometric AROM from 1,892 subjects,
  analysis limited to 1,313 white and 370 black subjects. Normative values "differ from
  estimates found in textbooks by as much as 18 degrees". Differences in mean AROM between the
  youngest and oldest age groups are "generally small, ranging from 3 to 5 degrees", and with
  one exception all motions were lower in the oldest group. **The abstract never states the age
  brackets** — it says only "youngest"/"oldest" and "to age 74 years". An earlier version of
  this entry gave them as 25-39 and 60-74; that precision was invented and is removed. Only hip extension declines by more than 20% of its arc. Conclusion: to age 74,
  substantial loss of mobility should be treated as abnormal rather than attributed to ageing.
- **Does not support** — any specific per-motion normative figure. The tables are paywalled.
  Quoting "hip flexion 121°" from this paper would be a fabrication; the abstract carries the
  *comparison* to textbook values, not the values.

## Hip osteoarthritis

### Sutlive TG, et al. Development of a clinical prediction rule for diagnosing hip osteoarthritis in individuals with unilateral hip pain.
*J Orthop Sports Phys Ther* 2008;38(9):542-550 · PMID 18758047 · **abstract only**

- **Supports** — prospective cohort, 72 subjects with unilateral hip pain; 21 (29%) had
  radiographic hip OA (Kellgren–Lawrence ≥ 2 on standing AP pelvis). A 5-variable rule; with
  at least 4 of 5 present, **+LR 24.3 (95% CI 4.4–142.1)**, raising probability to 91%.
  Authors call it preliminary and say a validation study is needed before clinical use.
- **Does not support** — **the identity of the five variables.** They are not in the free
  abstract. Listing them from memory is exactly the failure this file exists to prevent.

## Femoroacetabular impingement

### Reiman MP, Mather RC, Cook CE. Physical examination tests for hip dysfunction and injury.
*Br J Sports Med* 2015;49(6):357-361 · PMID 25736298 · **abstract only**

- **Supports** — that a systematic review with meta-analysis identified the hip tests of
  strongest diagnostic accuracy, and that this paper describes them; that diagnostic
  interpretation "requires careful consideration, with special attention to specific variables
  such as test performance and patient population".
- **Does not support** — any Sn/Sp/LR figure. This is the descriptive companion paper. The
  numbers live in the meta-analysis, located and read below (Reiman 2015, BJSM 49(12):811).

### Reiman MP, Goode AP, Cook CE, Hölmich P, Thorborg K. Diagnostic accuracy of clinical tests for the diagnosis of hip femoroacetabular impingement/labral tear: a systematic review with meta-analysis.
*Br J Sports Med* 2015;49(12):811 · PMID 25515771 · **abstract only**

This is the meta-analysis the 2015 companion paper above points at, and the only source in this
bank carrying pooled accuracy figures for the impingement tests. Everything below is in the free
abstract.

- **Supports** — 21 potential articles, **one of high quality**; nine qualified for meta-analysis.
  Random-effects pooling.
  **FADIR (flexion-adduction-internal rotation):** pooled SN **0.94 (95% CI 0.90–0.97) to 0.99
  (95% CI 0.98–1.00)**; DOR **5.71 (95% CI 0.84–38.86) to 7.82 (95% CI 1.06–57.84)**.
  **Flexion-internal rotation:** pooled SN **0.96 (95% CI 0.81–0.99)**; DOR **8.36 (95% CI
  0.41–171.3)**.
  The authors' own verdict on both, verbatim: they "possess only **screening accuracy**". And on
  the field as a whole: "Few hip physical examination tests for diagnosing FAI/ALT have been
  investigated in enough studies of substantial quality to direct clinical decision-making."
- **Does not support** — **any pooled specificity**, for any test: the abstract reports SN and DOR
  only. Nor **any figure for FABER**, which this abstract does not mention at all. Nor a
  confirmatory role for FADIR: both DOR intervals include or nearly include 1, which is what
  "screening accuracy" means here — a negative FADIR argues against, a positive one settles
  nothing. Writing a specificity next to these sensitivities is the exact failure this file exists
  to catch.

### Owusu-Akyaw KA, et al. Concurrent validity of a patient self-administered examination and a clinical examination for femoroacetabular impingement syndrome.
*BMJ Open Sport Exerc Med* 2019;5(1):e000574 · PMC6797256 · **free full text**

- **Supports** — 75 patients with hip-related pain; both a self-administered and a
  standardised clinical examination. Diagnostic accuracy of individual manoeuvres "varied
  widely"; both showed "no to moderate change in post-test probability" for FAIS. Mean
  diagnostic accuracy **53.6% self-exam vs 45.5% clinician exam** (p=0.02), which the authors
  themselves call low and "consistent with hip exam for FAIS reported in the literature".
- **Useful for** — the honest framing that the hip impingement examination performs poorly,
  stated by a study that set out to validate an examination.

### Reiman MP, et al. Diagnostic accuracy of imaging modalities and injection techniques for the diagnosis of femoroacetabular impingement/labral tear.
*Am J Sports Med* 2017;45(11):2665-2677 · PMID 28129509 · **abstract only**

- Located, not yet read for figures. Imaging and injection, not physical examination.

### Reiman MP, Thorborg K. Clinical examination and physical assessment of hip joint-related pain in athletes.
*Int J Sports Phys Ther* 2014;9(6):737-755 · PMC4223284 · **free full text**

- Located, free, not yet read. Likely source for examination description and context.

## Muscle length

### Willett GM, Keim SA, Shostrom VK, Lomneth CS. An anatomic investigation of the Ober test.
*Am J Sports Med* 2016;44(3):696-701 · PMID 26755689 · **abstract only**

The hip's equivalent of the shoulder's can tests: a named test that does not do what its name
says. Everything below is in the free abstract.

- **Supports** — cadaveric, sequential transection. Ober n=28, modified Ober n=34; 18 limbs
  taken through every condition (intact → ITB midthigh → gluteus medius/minimus → hip joint
  capsule). **No significant change in adduction after ITB transection** in either version.
  Significant change for intact vs gluteus med/min and intact vs hip capsule (both P < .0001),
  and between those two conditions as well (Ober P < .0001; modified Ober P = .0036).
  Modified Ober means: **5.00°** after ITB transection, **11.20°** after gluteus med/min,
  **13.20°** after capsule. Ober means: **−2.20°**, **6.50°**, **9.53°**.
  Authors' conclusion, verbatim in the abstract: the findings "refute the hypothesis that the
  ITB plays a role in limiting hip adduction during either version of the Ober test and
  question the validity of these tests for determining ITB tightness"; the test "assesses
  tightness of structures proximal to the hip joint … rather than the ITB".
- **Does not support** — any claim about living subjects, symptomatic hips, or reliability.
  This is a cadaveric anatomic study.

## Muscle length — reliability

### González-de-la-Flor Á, Seijas-Otero D. Reliability of physical examination tests for assessing hip flexor flexibility: a systematic review and meta-analysis.
*PM&R* 2026;18(7):779-792 · PMID 41420517 · PMC13358456 · **free full text**

- **Supports** — 27 studies, 1,056 participants; random-effects pooling of ICCs.
  Overall pooled **intrarater ICC 0.90 (95% CI 0.85–0.95)** (n=888, "excellent");
  **interrater ICC 0.80 (95% CI 0.71–0.88)** (n=665, "good").
  But heterogeneity is near-total — **I² = 99% intrarater, 98.3% interrater** — and worst for
  the modified Thomas and Thomas tests specifically. Within the subgroup analysis the
  **classic Thomas test had the lowest interrater reliability of any test: ICC 0.62 (95% CI
  0.46–0.78)**. Authors' conclusion: reliability is generally high but "considerable
  methodological variability was identified, particularly for the MTT and Thomas Test,
  suggesting a need for standardized protocols".
- **Does not support** — validity of any of these tests, i.e. whether they measure hip flexor
  length at all; this review measures agreement only. Nor the newer Falcon and Swing tests as
  usable: the authors say those "require further validation". Nor a single headline ICC for
  "the Thomas test" — the pooled 0.90 is across five different tests, and quoting it as the
  Thomas test's reliability inverts the paper's own finding about that test.

## Capsular pattern

### Bijl D, Dekker J, van Baar ME, Oostendorp RA, Lemmens AM, Bijlsma JW, Voorn TB. Validity of Cyriax's concept capsular pattern for the diagnosis of osteoarthritis of hip and/or knee.
*Scand J Rheumatol* 1998;27(5):347-351 · PMID 9808397 · **abstract only**

- **Supports** — 200 patients. The capsular pattern of the hip, as Cyriax describes it
  (limited medial rotation, flexion and abduction), "was not present as a distinct pattern in
  patients with OA of the hip". For the knee there was an indication of a pattern (flexion and
  extension) in subgroups. Conclusion: the capsular pattern "cannot be regarded as a valid
  test for the diagnosis of OA of the hip or knee".
- **Does not support** — that the capsular pattern is useless as a description, or anything
  about non-arthritic stiffness. It tested the pattern as a *diagnostic* for OA.

## Hip abductor function, Trendelenburg and GTPS

These two papers disagree, and the disagreement is the content. One measures the Trendelenburg
sign against MRI in symptomatic patients and finds it the best of three signs; the other paralyses
the muscles it is supposed to test and finds it barely responds. A guide that quotes only the
first is quoting half a literature.

### Bird PA, Oakley SP, Shnier R, Kirkham BW. Prospective evaluation of magnetic resonance imaging and physical examination findings in patients with greater trochanteric pain syndrome.
*Arthritis Rheum* 2001;44(9):2138-2145 · PMID 11592379 · **abstract only**

- **Supports** — 24 subjects with clinical GTPS, all women, median age 58 (range 36–75), median
  symptom duration 12 months. MRI: **11 (45.8%) gluteus medius tear**, **15 (62.5%) gluteus medius
  tendinitis** (pure in 9, with tear in 6), **2 trochanteric bursal distension**, 1 AVN.
  Of three signs tested, **Trendelenburg's sign was the most accurate for predicting a tendon
  tear: sensitivity 72.7%, specificity 76.9%**, and the most reliable: **intraobserver kappa 0.676
  (95% CI 0.270–1.08)** — measured in **12 of the 24 patients**, re-assessed at 2 months by the
  same observer, not in the whole sample. Authors' conclusion: gluteus medius tendon pathology is what defines
  GTPS; bursal distension was uncommon and never occurred without gluteus medius pathology.
- **Does not support** — GTPS as a bursitis; this paper is the evidence against that name.
  Nor does it support the accuracy figures generalising: n=24, all female, single radiologist,
  and the reliability kappa's confidence interval runs from 0.270 to above 1, i.e. from poor to
  uninformative. The other two signs (pain on resisted abduction, pain on resisted internal
  rotation) have no figures in the free abstract.

### Schmid M, Hoch A, Dimitriou D, Rosner J, Schubert M, Eichenberger U, Zingg PO. Evaluation of Trendelenburg and Duchenne signs by experimentally induced gluteal muscle paralysis.
*Clin Biomech* 2026;139:106931 · PMID 42501605 · **abstract only**

- **Supports** — ten healthy adults, sequential nerve blocks of the superior gluteal branch to
  tensor fasciae latae, then to gluteus medius and minimus, then the inferior gluteal nerve to
  gluteus maximus; ten-second single-leg stance captured with 56 markers and 27 cameras.
  After **gluteus medius and minimus paralysis, 5 of 10 could not hold single-leg stance for ten
  seconds and only one showed a Trendelenburg sign.** After gluteus maximus paralysis, 5 of 10
  failed and two showed a Duchenne sign. Authors' conclusion, verbatim: the signs "lack both
  sensitivity and specificity and should not be solely attributed to gluteus medius and minimus
  weakness", and gluteus maximus impairment "may also produce similar compensatory patterns or
  mask abductor deficits".
- **Does not support** — any accuracy figure against a clinical diagnosis; this is an
  experimental paralysis model in healthy volunteers, n=10, not a diagnostic study.

**How this goes in the guide:** `Contested`. Trendelenburg's accuracy is measured and the studies
disagree, so the guide quotes both and the range, rather than picking the flattering one. What
both agree on is that a positive sign does not localise to gluteus medius.

## Hip osteoarthritis — the clinical practice guideline

### Koc TA, Cibulka M, Enseki KR, Gentile JT, MacDonald CW, Kollmorgen RC, Martin RL. Hip Pain and Mobility Deficits — Hip Osteoarthritis: Revision 2025.
*J Orthop Sports Phys Ther* 2025;55(11):CPG1-CPG31 · PMID 41165671 ·
**abstract only in Europe PMC, but the full 31-page guideline is openly hosted by the Academy of
Orthopaedic Physical Therapy** at `orthopt.org` — so it is readable for free and quotable in
full. Everything below was read from that PDF.

This is the single most useful source in the bank: it carries graded recommendations, so a value
can be quoted *with its strength of evidence attached* rather than asserted flat.

**Grades, from the guideline's own Table 2** — needed for any letter quoted below to mean
anything. **A** strong (a preponderance of level I/II studies, must include ≥1 level I;
obligation "must or should"). **B** moderate (a single high-quality RCT or a preponderance of
level II; "should"). **C** weak (a single level II study, or a preponderance of level III–IV
including expert consensus; "may"). **D** conflicting (higher-quality studies disagree).
**E** theoretical/foundational (animal, cadaver, conceptual). **F** expert opinion.

#### Diagnosis — **Grade A** (2017 recommendation, retained in 2025)
Classify adults **over 50** into ICD coxarthrosis / ICF hip pain (b28016) and mobility deficits
(b7100) using: moderate anterior or lateral hip pain **during weight-bearing activities**;
morning stiffness **less than 1 hour** after waking; **hip IR ROM less than 24°**, *or* IR and
hip flexion **15° less than the nonpainful side**; and/or increased hip pain with **passive hip
IR**.

- This is where the guide's hip IR number comes from, and it is a *diagnostic threshold in
  people over 50*, not a normative range. It must not be presented as "normal hip IR".

#### Physical impairment measures — **Grade A**
Document the **FABER (Patrick's) test**, passive hip ROM, and hip muscle strength in **IR, ER,
flexion, extension, abduction and adduction**.

- Note where the 2025 Essential Data Elements put FABER: under **joint irritability**, not under
  diagnosis. That is the hip's answer to the shoulder guide's irritability framework, and it
  comes from the guideline rather than from convention.

#### Activity and performance measures — **Grade A**
Self-report: WOMAC Physical Function subscale, HOOS, PROMIS. Performance: **6MWT, 30-s
chair-stand, TUG, timed stair performance**. Balance and falls (**Grade A**): Berg Balance
Scale, four-square step test, timed single-leg stance. Fall-risk management per APTA Geriatrics
(**Grade F**).

#### Interventions, with grades and doses
- **Manual therapy — A.** Soft tissue and/or joint mobilization, "including high- and low-force
  long-axis hip distraction and hip mobilization with movement", for mild-to-moderate hip OA.
  Dose (2017, retained): **1–3 times per week for 6–12 weeks**. As motion improves, add
  stretching and strengthening to sustain the gains.
- **Manual therapy modification — F.** Modify procedures and force amplitude to the patient's
  **bony hip morphology and tissue tolerance/irritability**.
- **Exercise — A.** Individualized, potentially including aquatic therapy, for motion, strength,
  function and pain. Dose: **1–5 times per week, 30–120 min per session, over 5–16 weeks**.
- **Dry needling — A.** Myofascial trigger points in **iliopsoas, rectus femoris, TFL, gluteus
  medius, gluteus minimus**, for **short-term (3 weeks)** gains, in **K-L Grades II and III**.
- **Patient education — B.** Activity modification, exercise, weight reduction where overweight,
  joint unloading, and internet-based pain coping skills training with exercise and/or manual
  therapy.
- **Weight loss — B.** Collaborate with physicians, nutritionists or dietitians.
- **Functional, gait and balance training — C.** Impairment-based, including assistive devices,
  where problems are observed and documented. Individualize to the patient's values and
  participation needs (also **C**).
- **Ultrasound — D (conflicting).** May be added under shared decision making; the patient must
  be told the evidence conflicts and about any cost.
- **Bracing — F.** **Not** first line. May be used after exercise or manual therapy fail, for
  turning/pivoting activities in mild-to-moderate and especially bilateral hip OA.

#### Also carried, and citable
- **Risk factors** (2017 summary): age, history of hip developmental disorders, previous hip
  injury, **reduced hip ROM especially IR**, osteophytes, lower socioeconomic status, higher
  bone mass, higher BMI.
- **Kellgren–Lawrence grades 0–4**, each defined verbatim in the guideline (0 none; 1 doubtful
  narrowing, possible osteophyte; 2 possible narrowing and osteophyte; 3 definite narrowing,
  moderate osteophyte, some sclerosis, possible bone-end deformity; 4 large osteophyte, severe
  narrowing, marked sclerosis, definite deformity).
- **Imaging** (2025 update): radiographs of pelvis and hip usually appropriate first, per
  American College of Radiology "chronic hip pain" criteria; US, then MRI/CT, if inconclusive.

- **Does not support** — normative hip ROM for a healthy population; every ROM figure here is a
  diagnostic threshold in suspected OA. Nor accuracy figures (Sn/Sp/LR) for FABER or any other
  test: the guideline recommends documenting FABER, it does not report how well it discriminates.

## What could not be traced, and what the guide will therefore say

These are not gaps left for later. Each one was searched for and not found in anything a reader
can reach for free, and that determination is itself content — the guide states it in place
rather than quietly presenting a number as though it were sourced.

### Per-motion normative hip ROM — **Convention**
Searched Europe PMC for free normative goniometric hip ROM in a general adult population. What
comes back is athlete cohorts (soccer, track and field) and post-surgical series, not norms. The
classic sources — Boone & Azen, the AAOS and AMA tables, and Roach & Miles itself — are
paywalled at the table.

So every "normal hip flexion is 120°" figure in circulation is taught everywhere and traceable
nowhere free. It goes in marked `Convention`, and Roach 1991 is cited beside it for the reason
that matters: measured against a national probability sample, textbook values were off by **as
much as 18°**. A student who has memorised one number should know it is a convention with a
known error bar, not a measurement.

The one hip ROM figure the guide can state unmarked is **hip IR less than 24°**, from the Koc
2025 CPG — and only as a *diagnostic threshold in adults over 50*, never as a normal value.

### The five variables of the Sutlive prediction rule — **untraceable**
The +LR of 24.3 is in the free abstract; the identity of the five variables is not. The 2025 CPG
does not mention Sutlive, the 2009 CPG's full text is not released for text mining, and no free
validation study restating them was found. The guide will quote the rule's performance and say
plainly that the variables themselves need the paywalled paper — rather than listing five
plausible ones from memory, which is precisely how the shoulder draft acquired a fabricated
citation.

### Leg-length measurement reliability — **not found free**
Searches return post-arthroplasty radiographic and spinopelvic work, not tape-measure reliability
in a clinical setting. Until a free source turns up, leg-length measurement gets no reliability
figure at all.

### FABER diagnostic accuracy — **not found**
Absent from the FAI clinical-test meta-analysis (Reiman 2015 does not mention it) and absent from
the CPG, which recommends documenting FABER at Grade A without reporting how well it
discriminates. The guide will present FABER as the CPG does — an **irritability** measure — and
say that its diagnostic accuracy is not established, which is the more useful fact anyway.
