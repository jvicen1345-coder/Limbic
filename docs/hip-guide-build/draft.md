# Hip Examination Playbook — draft

**Status: draft. Not verified. Not built. Do not publish.**

Written against `sources.md` and nothing else. Every value below is one of four things, and the
draft marks which:

- **unmarked** — the named paper's freely readable text carries that exact value
- **`Convention`** — taught everywhere, measured by nobody, or measured only behind a paywall
- **`Contested`** — measured, and the studies disagree; the range is quoted
- **`No traceable source`** — stated in place, rather than presented as though sourced

Procedure is not a value. How to position a limb needs no citation; the number you compare the
result against does.

Section order matches the shoulder guide: checklist · numbers · posture · AROM · PROM & length ·
MMT · special tests · syndromes · palpation · mobs & exercise · drill · refs.

---

## 1 · Checklist — the examination in order

*Lede: the order is the content. A hip screen that starts with special tests has already skipped
the two questions that decide whether the special tests mean anything.*

| Prompt | Answer |
|---|---|
| Before you touch the hip | Screen the lumbar spine and SI joint. Hip and lumbar pain refer into each other's territory, and a hip examination performed on a referred pain will be normal and reassuring and wrong. |
| Red flags that change the plan | Fracture, slipped capital femoral epiphysis in the adolescent, avascular necrosis, septic arthritis, malignancy. SCFE is the one that is missed: an adolescent with knee pain and a limp gets a hip examined. |
| Classify from the history first | Intra-articular, extra-articular, or referred. This decides which tests you run — not the other way round. |
| Resting state | Standing posture and pelvic alignment, leg length, muscle bulk. |
| What they can do alone | Gait, single-leg stance, squat, active range in all planes, then a functional composite (step-down, sit-to-stand). |
| What you do to them | Passive range against active, end feel, capsular pattern, accessory motion. |
| What is short | Thomas, Ober, hamstring length, piriformis length. |
| What is weak | Abductors, extensors, flexors and adductors, graded 0–5 — and weakness distinguished from inhibition. |
| Where is it | Bony then soft-tissue palpation: greater trochanter, adductor origin, ischial tuberosity. |
| What is it | Special tests by category, interpreted as likelihood ratios, clustered from your hypothesis rather than run as a battery. |
| What you call it | A movement diagnosis, the syndrome it matches, and where the relative stiffness sits. |
| What you do | Dose from irritability, match intervention to impairment, mobilize with a stated rationale, and name the reassessment marker before you treat. |

## 2 · Numbers — and what each one is worth

*Lede: this is the section that separates this guide from a revision card. Most hip numbers a
student memorises have no traceable primary source, and the two that do are diagnostic thresholds
rather than norms. Both facts are more useful than the numbers.*

| Prompt | Answer |
|---|---|
| Normal hip ROM, by plane | **`Convention`.** Every circulating figure — flexion 120°, abduction 45°, IR 45° — is taught everywhere and traceable nowhere a reader can reach for free. The classic tables (Boone & Azen, AAOS, AMA) are paywalled at the table. |
| Why that matters more than the number | Roach & Miles measured active hip and knee ROM in a national probability sample (NHANES I, 1,892 subjects; analysis of 1,313 white and 370 black subjects) and found normative values **differ from textbook estimates by as much as 18°**. `src: Roach 1991` |
| Does hip range fall with age? | Barely. Between the youngest and oldest age groups, mean AROM differences are "generally small, ranging from 3 to 5 degrees" — and with one exception all motions were lower in the oldest group. Only **hip extension** declines by more than 20% of its arc. To age 74, substantial loss of mobility is abnormal, not ageing. `src: Roach 1991` |
| The one ROM figure you can state as sourced | **Hip IR less than 24°** — or IR and flexion 15° less than the unaffected side — is part of the Grade A diagnostic criteria for hip OA. `src: Koc 2025` |
| …with the caveat that makes it usable | That is a **diagnostic threshold in adults over 50**, not a normal value. A 25-year-old with 20° of IR has not met a criterion; they have a hip that needs explaining. |
| Full Grade A criteria for hip OA | Adults **over 50** with: moderate anterior or lateral hip pain **on weight-bearing**; morning stiffness **under 1 hour**; hip **IR < 24°** *or* IR and flexion 15° less than the painless side; and/or increased pain with **passive IR**. `src: Koc 2025` |
| FADIR — how good is it? | Pooled sensitivity **0.94 (95% CI 0.90–0.97) to 0.99 (0.98–1.00)**; diagnostic odds ratio **5.71 (0.84–38.86) to 7.82 (1.06–57.84)**. The authors' verdict: it possesses **"only screening accuracy"**. `src: Reiman 2015` |
| What that means at the bedside | A negative FADIR argues meaningfully against impingement. A positive one settles nothing — the DOR intervals include or nearly include 1. `src: Reiman 2015` |
| FADIR specificity | **`No traceable source`.** The meta-analysis reports sensitivity and DOR only. Any specificity quoted beside those sensitivities has been invented. |
| Flexion–internal rotation test | Pooled sensitivity **0.96 (95% CI 0.81–0.99)**; DOR **8.36 (0.41–171.3)**. Same conclusion: screening only. `src: Reiman 2015` |
| How much evidence is behind all that | 21 candidate articles, **one of high quality**; nine entered the meta-analysis. The authors state plainly that few hip tests "have been investigated in enough studies of substantial quality to direct clinical decision-making". `src: Reiman 2015` |
| FABER accuracy | **`No traceable source`.** Absent from the FAI meta-analysis and absent from the CPG's evidence — while the CPG still recommends documenting it at Grade A, filed under **joint irritability**, not diagnosis. Treat it as an irritability measure. `src: Reiman 2015` `src: Koc 2025` |
| How well does the hip exam perform overall? | In 75 patients examined for FAIS, mean diagnostic accuracy was **53.6%** for a patient self-examination and **45.5%** for a standardised clinician examination (p=0.02) — both described by the authors as low and "consistent with hip exam for FAIS reported in the literature". `src: Owusu-Akyaw 2019` |
| Hip OA clinical prediction rule | With at least 4 of 5 variables present, **+LR 24.3 (95% CI 4.4–142.1)**, raising probability to 91%. From 72 patients with unilateral hip pain, 21 (29%) with radiographic OA (Kellgren–Lawrence ≥ 2). The authors call it preliminary and say it needs validation before clinical use. `src: Sutlive 2008` |
| The five variables themselves | **`No traceable source`.** They are not in the free abstract, the 2025 CPG does not cite Sutlive, and no free validation restates them. Quote the rule's performance; do not list variables you cannot read. |
| Trendelenburg sign accuracy | **`Contested`.** Against MRI in 24 symptomatic patients: sensitivity **72.7%**, specificity **76.9%**, the best of three signs tested. `src: Bird 2001` But under experimental paralysis of gluteus medius and minimus, **only 1 of 10** healthy adults produced the sign. `src: Schmid 2026` |
| Trendelenburg reliability | Intraobserver kappa **0.676**, 95% CI **0.270–1.08** — an interval running from poor to uninformative. Measured in a **12-patient subset** of the 24, re-tested at 2 months by the same observer. `src: Bird 2001` |
| Thomas test reliability | Pooled across five hip-flexor tests: intrarater ICC **0.90 (0.85–0.95)**, interrater **0.80 (0.71–0.88)**. But heterogeneity is **I² = 99% / 98.3%**, and the **classic Thomas test has the lowest interrater reliability of any of them: ICC 0.62 (0.46–0.78)**. `src: González-de-la-Flor 2026` |
| The trap in that number | The headline 0.90 is across five different tests. Quoting it as "the Thomas test's reliability" inverts the paper's own finding about that test. `src: González-de-la-Flor 2026` |
| Imaging, if you are wondering | CT arthrography is the most accurate for labral tear: SN **0.91 (0.83–0.96)**, SP **0.89 (0.74–0.97)**, +LR **6.28**, −LR **0.11**. But pretest probability in those studies was **81%** for labral tear and **74%** for FAI, which the authors say limits generalisability sharply. `src: Reiman 2017` |
| Leg-length measurement reliability | **`No traceable source`.** No free clinical tape-measure reliability study was found. Measure it, record it, and do not attach a confidence figure you cannot support. |

## 3 · Posture and resting alignment

*Lede: the resting state is the baseline every later finding is compared against. It is also where
the least is measured — most of what is taught here is convention, and saying so is the point.*

| Prompt | Answer |
|---|---|
| Standing pelvic alignment | Observe iliac crest heights, ASIS and PSIS levels, and frontal-plane pelvic tilt. **`Convention`** — the thresholds that make an asymmetry "significant" are not established in a free source. |
| Leg length — how to measure | Supine, ASIS to medial malleolus, both sides, same landmarks. Record the difference. |
| Leg length — what the difference means | **`No traceable source`** for the clinical reliability of this measurement, and therefore none for a cut-off. Treat an apparent discrepancy as a finding to corroborate, not a diagnosis. |
| Muscle bulk | Compare gluteal and quadriceps bulk side to side. Atrophy is a chronicity marker, not a diagnosis. **`Convention`.** |
| What the resting state is actually for | Hip OA risk factors include reduced hip ROM — especially IR — as well as age, previous hip injury, developmental hip disorders, osteophytes, higher BMI, higher bone mass, and lower socioeconomic status. `src: Koc 2025` |

## 4 · Active range and what they can do alone

*Lede: active movement is where you learn what the hip is willing to do, and single-leg stance is
the most informative ten seconds of the examination — as long as you do not over-read the sign it
produces.*

| Prompt | Answer |
|---|---|
| Active range, all planes | Flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, IR, ER. Compare sides before comparing to a book — the norms are `Convention` (§2) and the unaffected side is the better reference. |
| Which motion to weight most | IR. It is in the Grade A diagnostic criteria for hip OA and it is a listed risk factor for developing it. `src: Koc 2025` |
| Single-leg stance — what to watch | Pelvic drop on the unsupported side (Trendelenburg) and lateral trunk lean over the stance leg (Duchenne). Time the hold; ten seconds is the tested interval. `src: Schmid 2026` |
| What a positive Trendelenburg tells you | That the frontal-plane pelvic control has failed. **It does not localise to gluteus medius.** Under experimental paralysis of gluteus medius and minimus, 5 of 10 could not hold ten seconds but only 1 showed the sign; after gluteus maximus paralysis 5 of 10 also failed, 2 with a Duchenne sign. `src: Schmid 2026` |
| So is the sign useful? | **`Contested`.** In symptomatic patients it was the most accurate and most reliable of three signs for a gluteus medius tear (Sn 72.7%, Sp 76.9%). `src: Bird 2001` In an experimental model it barely responded to paralysis of the muscle it names. `src: Schmid 2026` Use it as a screen that something in the stance-limb chain is failing. |
| Gait | Observe for antalgia, trunk lean, pelvic drop, and reduced stance time. **`Convention`** — no free source gives accuracy figures for gait observation at the hip. |
| Squat | Depth, symmetry, and whether pain reproduces. `Convention`. |
| Functional composites | Step-down and sit-to-stand. Prefer the measures the CPG names at Grade A: **6-minute walk, 30-second chair stand, timed up-and-go, timed stair performance**. `src: Koc 2025` |
| Balance and falls | Grade A: measure balance and fall risk in adults with hip OA, especially with reduced function or a fall history — Berg Balance Scale, four-square step test, timed single-leg stance. `src: Koc 2025` |

## 5 · Passive range, end feel, and length

*Lede: two of the best-known hip tests in this section do not do what their names say. The Ober
test does not test the iliotibial band, and the hip capsular pattern is not a pattern. Both are
cadaveric or cohort findings, both are quotable, and both are usually taught the other way round.*

| Prompt | Answer |
|---|---|
| Passive against active | Compare each plane. A passive range that exceeds active points to weakness or inhibition; a passive range that matches a limited active one points to the joint or to true tissue shortening. `Convention`. |
| End feel | Capsular, bony, springy, empty, muscle guarding. **`Convention`** — the taxonomy is Cyriax's and is taught universally; no free source establishes its reliability at the hip. |
| The capsular pattern of the hip | **It does not hold.** In 200 patients, the pattern Cyriax describes for the hip — limited medial rotation, flexion and abduction — "was not present as a distinct pattern" in hip OA, and the authors conclude it "cannot be regarded as a valid test for the diagnosis of OA of the hip or knee". `src: Bijl 1998` |
| What that does and does not mean | It was tested as a **diagnostic** for OA and failed as one. It is not evidence about non-arthritic stiffness, and it does not stop the pattern being a useful description of what you found. |
| Knee, for contrast | The same study found an indication of a knee pattern (flexion and extension) in subgroups — so the finding is specific to the hip, not a blanket rejection. `src: Bijl 1998` |
| Accessory motion | Long-axis distraction, lateral distraction, anterior and posterior glide. Grade the resistance you feel and compare sides. `Convention`. |
| Thomas test — what it is for | Hip flexor extensibility: iliopsoas by thigh position, rectus femoris by knee angle, TFL by the abduction drift. |
| Thomas test — how far to trust it | Reliability is the problem, not the technique. Pooled across five hip-flexor tests intrarater ICC is **0.90** and interrater **0.80**, but the **classic Thomas test's interrater ICC is 0.62 (0.46–0.78)** — the lowest of any test in the review. Overall heterogeneity was I² 99% intrarater and 98.3% interrater, which the authors attribute particularly to the modified Thomas and Thomas tests. `src: González-de-la-Flor 2026` |
| Which version to use | The modified Thomas test, and the same version every time. The review's own conclusion is that the variability is methodological and calls for standardised protocols. `src: González-de-la-Flor 2026` |
| Is any of it valid? | **`No traceable source`.** The review measures agreement, not whether these tests measure hip flexor length at all. |
| Ober test — the received version | Taught as a test of iliotibial band tightness. |
| Ober test — what a cadaveric study found | **Transecting the ITB produced no significant change in hip adduction** in either the Ober or the modified Ober. Significant change came only from cutting gluteus medius/minimus and the hip capsule (both P < .0001). `src: Willett 2016` |
| The numbers | Modified Ober mean adduction: intact **4.28°** → **5.00°** after ITB transection → **11.20°** after gluteus medius/minimus → **13.20°** after capsule. Ober: intact **−2.90°** → **−2.20°** → **6.50°** → **9.53°**. The ITB step is the one that does nothing. `src: Willett 2016` |
| The authors' conclusion, verbatim | The findings "refute the hypothesis that the ITB plays a role in limiting hip adduction during either version of the Ober test and question the validity of these tests for determining ITB tightness"; the test "assesses tightness of structures proximal to the hip joint … rather than the ITB". `src: Willett 2016` |
| What to say to a patient, then | That the test showed restriction proximal to the hip — gluteus medius/minimus and capsule — not a tight ITB. |
| Limits of that evidence | Cadaveric, sequential transection (Ober n=28, modified Ober n=34, 18 limbs through all conditions). It says nothing about living, symptomatic hips or about reliability. `src: Willett 2016` |
| Hamstring length | Passive straight-leg raise or 90/90 knee extension. **`Convention`** — no free source gives a threshold or reliability figure at the hip. |
| Piriformis length | Flexion, adduction and internal rotation with the hip flexed. **`Convention`**, and note that this position is also FADIR — a positive may be impingement, not muscle length. |

## 6 · Strength

*Lede: the CPG tells you which strength to measure and at what grade of evidence. It does not
give you norms, and no free source does either — so the comparison is the other side and the
change over time.*

| Prompt | Answer |
|---|---|
| Which motions to test | **IR, ER, flexion, extension, abduction and adduction** — with passive ROM and the FABER test, this is the Grade A physical impairment set for hip pain and hip OA. `src: Koc 2025` |
| Abductors | Side-lying abduction against resistance, pelvis stabilised, or hand-held dynamometry. Compare sides. |
| Abductor strength norms | **`No traceable source`.** No free normative dataset was found. Use side-to-side difference and serial change. |
| Extensors | Prone hip extension with the knee flexed to bias gluteus maximus. `Convention`. |
| Grading | 0–5 manual muscle test. **`Convention`** — universal, and no free reliability figure at the hip is recorded in this bank. |
| Weakness or inhibition? | Retest after reducing pain — a grade that improves immediately was inhibition, not weakness. `Convention`, and a clinically important distinction the literature does not quantify. |
| Why the distinction matters here | Because a Trendelenburg sign that comes from pain inhibition and one that comes from a gluteus medius tear look identical in stance, and the sign itself does not separate them. `src: Schmid 2026` |

## 7 · Special tests

*Lede: the honest summary of this section is that the hip's special tests screen better than they
confirm, and that one high-quality study underpins the whole literature. Interpreting them as
likelihood ratios is not a nicety — it is the only way to use tests this weak without misleading
yourself.*

| Prompt | Answer |
|---|---|
| Run tests from a hypothesis | Classify from the history first, then choose the category: impingement, labral, intra-articular, extra-articular. A battery run on everyone produces false positives at the rate the prevalence dictates. |
| FADIR / anterior impingement | Flexion, adduction, internal rotation. Pooled SN **0.94–0.99**; DOR **5.71–7.82**; **"only screening accuracy"**. `src: Reiman 2015` |
| How to use FADIR | As a rule-out. Negative meaningfully lowers the probability of impingement; positive does not raise it much, and the DOR intervals include or nearly include 1. `src: Reiman 2015` |
| Flexion–internal rotation | Pooled SN **0.96 (0.81–0.99)**, DOR **8.36 (0.41–171.3)**. Same use, same caveat. `src: Reiman 2015` |
| FABER / Patrick's | Document it — Grade A. But the CPG lists it under **joint irritability**, not diagnosis, and no accuracy figure for it appears in either the CPG or the FAI meta-analysis. `src: Koc 2025` `src: Reiman 2015` |
| The quality behind these numbers | 21 candidate studies, **one** rated high quality; nine pooled. `src: Reiman 2015` |
| What the whole examination is worth | Mean diagnostic accuracy for FAIS: **53.6%** self-examination, **45.5%** standardised clinical examination. `src: Owusu-Akyaw 2019` |
| Hip OA — the strongest cluster | ≥4 of 5 variables: **+LR 24.3 (4.4–142.1)**, post-test probability 91%, in a sample with 29% prevalence. Preliminary and unvalidated by the authors' own statement. `src: Sutlive 2008` The five variables are **`No traceable source`** — see §2. |
| Interpreting a likelihood ratio | **`Convention`.** The familiar bands — +LR over 10 or −LR under 0.1 as decisive, 2–5 as a small shift — are a widely taught heuristic, not a value traced to any source in this bank. What *is* sourced: a high sensitivity with a DOR interval crossing 1, as here, is a screening test and nothing more. `src: Reiman 2015` |
| Building a cluster | Combine tests that fail in different ways. Two tests that share a mechanism — FADIR and a piriformis length test share a position — do not give independent information. |

## 8 · Syndromes and what you call it

*Lede: naming matters here more than at most joints, because the most common name in this section
is wrong. "Trochanteric bursitis" describes a bursa that is usually not the problem.*

| Prompt | Answer |
|---|---|
| Greater trochanteric pain syndrome | In 24 patients with clinical GTPS: **11 (45.8%) had a gluteus medius tear**, **15 (62.5%) had gluteus medius tendinitis** (pure in 9, with tear in 6), and only **2 had trochanteric bursal distension** — which never occurred without gluteus medius pathology. `src: Bird 2001` |
| So what is GTPS? | A **gluteal tendinopathy**, not a bursitis. The authors' conclusion is that gluteus medius tendon pathology is what defines the syndrome. `src: Bird 2001` |
| How to examine for it | Trendelenburg was the most accurate of three signs for a tear (Sn 72.7%, Sp 76.9%, kappa 0.676). `src: Bird 2001` Read that against §4 — the sign does not localise. |
| Limits | n=24, all women, median age 58 (range 36–75), single radiologist — and the reliability kappa comes from a 12-patient subset. Do not present those figures as settled. `src: Bird 2001` |
| Hip osteoarthritis | Adults over 50 meeting the Grade A criteria in §2. `src: Koc 2025` |
| Radiographic grading | Kellgren–Lawrence 0–4: 0 none; 1 doubtful narrowing, possible osteophyte; 2 possible narrowing and osteophyte; 3 definite narrowing, moderate osteophyte, some sclerosis, possible bone-end deformity; 4 large osteophyte, severe narrowing, marked sclerosis, definite deformity. `src: Koc 2025` |
| Femoroacetabular impingement | Screened by FADIR and flexion-IR, confirmed by neither. `src: Reiman 2015` Expect the examination to perform around chance-adjacent levels. `src: Owusu-Akyaw 2019` |
| Movement diagnosis | State the movement fault and the direction, not just the tissue: what the hip does under load that reproduces the symptom. |
| Relative stiffness | Where the motion is coming from instead. A hip that will not extend produces lumbar extension; a hip that will not rotate produces it at the pelvis or the knee. `Convention`. |
| When to change your mind | Grade F: revise the diagnosis, change the plan, or refer when the history, activity limitations or impairments do not fit the classification, or when symptoms are not improving with treatment aimed at the impairments. `src: Koc 2025` |

## 9 · Palpation

*Lede: palpation comes late deliberately. Palpating first tells you where a patient is tender;
palpating after the movement examination tells you whether that tenderness explains anything.*

| Prompt | Answer |
|---|---|
| Bony landmarks | Iliac crest, ASIS, PSIS, greater trochanter, ischial tuberosity, pubic tubercle. |
| Greater trochanter | Tenderness here is the entry point to GTPS — which is a gluteal tendinopathy far more often than a bursitis. `src: Bird 2001` |
| Adductor origin | Pubic tubercle and the adductor tendons. `Convention`. |
| Ischial tuberosity | Proximal hamstring origin; tender in proximal hamstring tendinopathy, and reproduced by sitting. `Convention`. |
| What palpation cannot do | Separate a gluteus medius tear from tendinitis — MRI was needed for that distinction, and tendinitis and tear coexisted in 6 of 24. `src: Bird 2001` |
| Accuracy of hip palpation | **`No traceable source`.** No free source in this bank reports sensitivity, specificity or reliability for palpation at the hip. |

## 10 · Mobilisation, exercise and dose

*Lede: this is the best-evidenced section in the guide, because a 2025 clinical practice guideline
carries graded recommendations with doses attached. Every letter below is defined — a value quoted
with its strength of evidence is worth more than a value quoted alone.*

**Grades, from the guideline's own table.** **A** strong (a preponderance of level I/II studies,
including at least one level I; obligation "must or should"). **B** moderate (a single
high-quality RCT or a preponderance of level II; "should"). **C** weak (a single level II, or a
preponderance of level III–IV including expert consensus; "may"). **D** conflicting. **E**
theoretical or foundational. **F** expert opinion. `src: Koc 2025`

| Prompt | Answer |
|---|---|
| Set the dose from irritability | The CPG's own irritability measure at the hip is the **FABER test**, listed among the essential data elements under joint irritability. `src: Koc 2025` |
| Manual therapy — the recommendation | **Grade A.** Soft tissue and/or joint mobilisation, "including high- and low-force long-axis hip distraction and hip mobilisation with movement", to increase ROM, decrease pain and improve function in mild-to-moderate hip OA. `src: Koc 2025` |
| Manual therapy — the dose | **1–3 times per week for 6–12 weeks**. As motion improves, add stretching and strengthening to sustain the gains. `src: Koc 2025` |
| Mobilising with a stated rationale | **Grade F:** modify procedure and force amplitude to the patient's **bony hip morphology and tissue tolerance/irritability**. The rationale is the morphology and the irritability, not the grade number. `src: Koc 2025` |
| Exercise — the recommendation | **Grade A.** An individualised programme, potentially including aquatic therapy, for motion, strength, function and pain. `src: Koc 2025` |
| Exercise — the dose | **1–5 times per week, 30–120 minutes per session, over 5–16 weeks**. `src: Koc 2025` |
| Dry needling | **Grade A.** Myofascial trigger points in **iliopsoas, rectus femoris, TFL, gluteus medius and gluteus minimus**, for **short-term (3-week)** gains in extensibility, pain, ROM, function and force production, in **K-L Grade II and III** hip OA. `src: Koc 2025` |
| Patient education | **Grade B.** Activity modification, exercise, weight reduction where overweight, joint unloading, and internet-based pain coping skills training combined with exercise and/or manual therapy. `src: Koc 2025` |
| Weight loss | **Grade B.** Collaborate with physicians, nutritionists or dietitians alongside exercise. `src: Koc 2025` |
| Functional, gait and balance training | **Grade C.** Impairment-based, including proper use of assistive devices, where limitations are observed and documented. Individualise to the patient's values and participation needs (also C). `src: Koc 2025` |
| Therapeutic ultrasound | **Grade D — conflicting.** May be added under shared decision making, but the patient must be told the evidence conflicts and about any cost. `src: Koc 2025` |
| Bracing | **Grade F. Not first line.** May be used after exercise and manual therapy have failed, for turning and pivoting activities, in mild-to-moderate and especially bilateral hip OA. `src: Koc 2025` |
| The reassessment marker | Name it before you treat. Use at least one self-report and one performance measure, plus the impairment set, at baseline and at least one follow-up. `src: Koc 2025` |
| Which measures | Self-report: **WOMAC Physical Function subscale, HOOS, PROMIS**. Performance: **6MWT, 30-second chair stand, TUG, timed stair performance**. Impairment: hip ROM and strength in all six directions, NPRS for pain, FABER for irritability. `src: Koc 2025` |
| Monitoring over time | Track ROM and strength, baseline pain, K-L grade, joint space width and outcome score. `src: Koc 2025` |

## 11 · Drill

*Recall prompts. Answers are the rows above; the drill exists so the guide is used rather than
read.*

1. What are the four Grade A diagnostic criteria for hip OA, and what age do they apply from?
2. Hip IR less than 24° — normal value or diagnostic threshold?
3. FADIR has a pooled sensitivity of up to 0.99. Why can you not use it to confirm impingement?
4. What is FADIR's pooled specificity?
5. What does the Ober test actually assess, and what does it not?
6. Which structures changed Ober adduction when transected, and by how much?
7. Is the hip capsular pattern a valid diagnostic for hip OA? In how many patients was that tested?
8. Give the Trendelenburg sign's sensitivity and specificity — then give the reason to distrust them.
9. After paralysing gluteus medius and minimus, how many of ten adults showed a Trendelenburg sign?
10. What is GTPS, in one word that is not "bursitis"? What proportion had bursal distension?
11. The pooled ICC for hip flexor length tests is 0.90. Why should you not quote that for the Thomas test?
12. State the manual therapy dose and the exercise dose for hip OA, with their grades.
13. Which intervention is Grade D, and what must you tell the patient?
14. Which is Grade F and not first line?
15. Which test does the CPG use as its irritability measure?
16. By how much did textbook hip ROM values differ from a national probability sample?

## 12 · References

Eleven sources, each cited at least once below. Every one was read in Europe PMC or, for the
CPG, in the openly hosted PDF. Full entries with a "supports / does not support" verdict are in
`sources.md`.

Two papers held in `sources.md` are deliberately **not** listed here. Reiman 2015 *Br J Sports Med*
49(6):357-361 is the descriptive companion to reference 3 and supports no value in this guide;
listing it would also put two `Reiman 2015` entries in the reference list, and the citation linker
matches on surname plus year, so every "Reiman 2015" in the text would resolve by list order
rather than by intent. Reiman 2014 *Int J Sports Phys Ther* 9(6):737-755 is located and free but
nothing here cites it. An orphaned reference is a defect; an ambiguous one is worse.

1. Roach KE, Miles TP. Normal hip and knee active range of motion: the relationship to age. *Phys Ther* 1991;71(9):656-665.
