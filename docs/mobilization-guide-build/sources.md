## The central question: where do the grades come from?

### Searched, not found — the origin, definition or reliability of the mobilization grading systems

Two targeted searches were run: one for the grading systems themselves (Maitland, Kaltenborn,
"grades of movement") together with reliability or validity, and one for whether clinicians can
**reproduce** a nominated grade — force or amplitude consistency between therapists. Neither
returned a usable source. Results were unrelated trials of mobilization *effects*, myotonometry,
and a neurosurgical elasticity paper.

The four guideline PDFs already held for the other builds were then searched directly, applying
this project's own rule about not trusting a database search alone:

| Guideline | Maitland | Kaltenborn | Defines a grade? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ankle 2021 | 1 mention | 0 | **No** — used as a label, not defined |
| Hip 2025 | 0 | 0 | No — uses a force scale instead |
| Knee 2018 | 0 | 0 | No |
| Achilles 2024 | 0 | 0 | No |
| TKA 2026 | 0 | 0 | No |

**So the grades are `Convention` with no traceable origin** unless a source turns up. They are
taught universally, used as vocabulary in the literature, and — as far as anything reachable here
shows — never defined or tested for reproducibility.

The ankle guideline's single mention is worth quoting because of what it *is*: while summarising a
meta-analysis, it refers to "anterior-to-posterior **Maitland grade III** joint mobilizations,
Mulligan talocrural mobilizations with movement, tibiofibular manipulations, and plantar massage"
having large significant effects on patient-oriented outcomes in chronic ankle instability. That
uses the grade as a name for someone else's intervention. It establishes nothing about what a
grade III is, or whether two clinicians would produce the same one.

## What the guidelines use instead of grades

### Koc TA, et al. Hip Pain and Mobility Deficits — Hip Osteoarthritis: Revision 2025.
*J Orthop Sports Phys Ther* 2025;55(11):CPG1-CPG31 · **free full text, openly hosted**

- **Supports — a defined force vocabulary, with outcomes attached.** In 60 patients with Grade III
  hip osteoarthritis (three sessions, 1-week follow-up), long-axis distraction was compared at
  **high force (stretching exceeding resistance)**, **medium force (stopping at the point of
  resistance)** and **low force (no resistance)**. High force increased ROM significantly (flexion
  10.6&deg;, extension 8.0&deg;, abduction 6.4&deg;, adduction 3.3&deg;, ER 5.6&deg;, IR 7.6&deg;)
  where low and medium force did not; **low force produced the largest effect on pain pressure
  threshold (d = 2.0)**, and high force the largest on physical function (d = 0.5-0.7).
- **Why this matters more than the grades** — this is a dose description that is **defined by where
  the force stops relative to tissue resistance**, is reproducible from the description alone, and
  has outcomes attached to each level. It does what the grading systems are supposed to do, without
  a numeral.
- **Does not support** — any mapping between these three force levels and Maitland or Kaltenborn
  grades. They are not the same scale and must not be presented as translations of each other.

### Martin RL, et al. Lateral Ankle Ligament Sprains Revision 2021.
*J Orthop Sports Phys Ther* 2021;51(4):CPG1-CPG80 · **free full text, openly hosted**

- **Supports** — **Grade A**: manual therapy including **graded joint mobilisations,
  manipulations, and weight-bearing and non-weight-bearing mobilisation with movement** to improve
  weight-bearing dorsiflexion and dynamic balance **in the short term** in chronic ankle
  instability. And **Grade A** in the acute phase: lymphatic drainage, soft tissue and joint
  mobilisation, and **anterior-to-posterior talar mobilisation within pain-free movement**.
- **Note the dose language.** "Within pain-free movement" and "in the short term" are the
  constraints the guideline actually specifies. Neither is a grade.
- **Does not support** — a definition of "graded", or how long "short term" is.

## Manual therapy across five guidelines — one table

Harvested from the five guideline PDFs already held, front matter first and era headers respected.
It answers a question none of the individual guides can: **how good is the evidence for manual
therapy, and does it depend on where you are working?**

| Guideline | Grade | What is recommended |
|---|---|---|
| **Hip OA 2025** | **A** | Soft tissue and/or joint mobilization, including high- and low-force long-axis distraction and mobilization with movement, to increase ROM, decrease pain and improve function in mild-to-moderate hip OA |
| **Hip OA 2025** | **F** | *May* modify procedures and **force amplitude** to the patient's bony hip morphology and tissue tolerance/irritability |
| **Lateral ankle sprain 2021** | **A** | Lymphatic drainage, active and passive soft tissue and joint mobilization, and anterior-to-posterior talar mobilization **within pain-free movement**, alongside exercise |
| **Lateral ankle sprain 2021** | **A** | Graded joint mobilizations, manipulations, and weight-bearing and non-weight-bearing mobilization with movement, to improve dorsiflexion and dynamic balance **in the short term**, in chronic instability |
| **Lateral ankle sprain 2021** | **B** | Multiple interventions *may* supplement balance training, combining exercise and manual therapy, guided by patient values and clinician judgement |
| **Total knee arthroplasty 2026** | *moderate* | *May* use manual therapy with exercise and/or devices to augment active-assistive exercise for ROM in the early postoperative period |
| **Total knee arthroplasty 2026** | *moderate* | *May* use manual therapy among several options to decrease pain, **time frame not specified** |
| **Achilles midportion 2024** | **F** | *May* use manual therapy directed at manipulating and/or mobilizing muscles, joints and connective tissues where there are **mobility deficits** |
| **Knee meniscal and cartilage 2018** | **&mdash;** | **No manual therapy recommendation at all.** |

### What the table says

**The evidence for manual therapy is regional, not general.** It reaches **Grade A** at the hip and
the ankle, drops to **expert opinion (F)** for Achilles tendinopathy, sits at *moderate* on a
different scale after knee arthroplasty, and is **absent entirely** from the knee meniscal and
cartilage guideline. A student taught "manual therapy is evidence-based" has been told something
that is true in two of these five documents and unsupported in a third.

**Note the two grading scales.** Four of these guidelines use letters A-F; the arthroplasty
guideline uses Evidence Quality high/moderate/low. They are not interchangeable and a *moderate* is
not a *B*. An earlier version of this harvest reported "no manual therapy recommendation" for the
arthroplasty guideline purely because the extraction looked for a letter prefix — the
recommendation was there in a different format. Recorded because it is the same class of error as
the talar tilt appendix hit: **the search found nothing and the absence was almost believed.**

**Where dose is specified, it is never by grade number.** Force amplitude relative to tissue
tolerance (hip), within pain-free movement (ankle), in the short term (ankle), early postoperative
period (arthroplasty), where mobility deficits exist (Achilles). Not one says "grade III".

## Can two clinicians reproduce the same accessory glide?

This is the question the grading search failed to answer. One paper reaches it — for a glide
*test*, not a graded treatment, but it is the closest measured thing that exists.

### Nguyen AP, Bogaerts H, Galerne C, Fourchet F. Reproducibility of a modified posterior talar glide test in ankle sprain conditions.
*J Man Manip Ther* 2026;34(2):171-177 · PMID 40635166 · PMC13251517 · **free full text**

Twenty-eight participants (chronic ankle instability, copers, healthy controls). Two raters — one
novice, one experienced — each measured each ankle twice, with and without a referential
horizontal plane.

- **Supports — one clinician is consistent with themselves.** Intra-rater reliability was excellent
  for both raters: novice ICC = 0.97 (SEM 2.2°, MDC 4.1°), experienced ICC = 0.94 (SEM 2.4°,
  MDC 4.3°). Experience did not separate them.
- **Supports — two clinicians agree much less.** Inter-rater reliability for the standard test was
  **poor to good, ICC = 0.64** (SEM 2.8°, MDC 4.7°). The same movement, measured by two people,
  loses most of the agreement it had within one person.
- **Supports — an external reference recovers some of it.** Adding a referential horizontal plane
  raised inter-rater reliability to moderate-to-good, ICC = 0.78 (SEM 2.3°, MDC 4.2°). What
  improved reproducibility was not skill or grading vocabulary; it was a measurable external
  datum.
- **Supports — the authors' own framing.** The background states the posterior talar glide test
  "is recommended for ankle sprain assessment, but it has limited scientific support."
- **Supports — a noise floor.** MDC ranged 3.3–4.7°. A difference smaller than roughly 4° between
  two clinicians is not a finding.

**Does not support.** This is a *test* measured in degrees of angular displacement, not a
mobilization dose, and not a grade. It says nothing about whether two clinicians nominating
"grade III" would apply the same force, because no force was measured and no grading system was
used. One joint, 28 participants, two raters, one centre. The conclusion — "reproducible enough to
be used in a clinical setting" — is the authors' judgement about the intra-rater figures; the
inter-rater ICC of 0.64 is the number a reader should carry.

## The concave-convex rule

### Searched, not found — a clinical critique of the rule

`TITLE:"convex-concave" OR TITLE:"concave-convex"` and the same terms in abstracts returns
**materials science almost exclusively**: metamaterials, laser ablation microstructure, cut-
resistant fabrics, seawall hydrodynamics, and one rhinoplasty paper on lateral crura deformity.
The phrase is not owned by orthopaedics. Absence of a critique from this search is therefore
weak evidence of anything — the same lesson as the knee guideline that was openly hosted while a
database search said nothing.

One clinical paper does reach it, and it is the only one.

### Scarvell JM, Hribar N, Galvin CR, Pickering MR, Perriman DM, Lynch JT, Smith PN. Analysis of Kneeling by Medical Imaging Shows the Femur Moves Back to the Posterior Rim of the Tibial Plateau, Prompting Review of the Concave-Convex Rule.
*Phys Ther* 2019;99(3):311-318 · PMID 30690574 · **abstract only**

Twenty-five healthy participants aged over 45 (13 male) knelt from upright kneeling to full
flexion with the foot free. Fluoroscopy at 30 frames per second was registered to a CT scan to
produce a moving three-dimensional model, analysed in six degrees of freedom.

- **Supports — the measured kinematics.** Mean flexion reached was 142 (6)°. Posterior femoral
  translation was coupled to flexion, r = 0.96. From 90° to 150° of flexion the femur translated
  **posteriorly by 36 (3) mm**, finishing 23 (3) mm posterior to the centre of the tibia. Over the
  same arc it externally rotated from 8 (6)° to 16 (5)°; flexion was coupled to rotation, r = 0.47.
  Abduction was under 3° and lateral translation under 3 mm.
- **Supports — that the authors raise the rule by name.** Their stated conclusion: "Deep flexion
  requires femoral posterior translation and external rotation. These findings invite review of the
  concave-convex rule as it might apply to manual therapy of the knee."

**Does not support.** It does not refute the rule, and the guide must not say it does — "invite
review" is the authors' own hedge and it is the strongest verb available. No mobilization was
performed and no patient was treated: this is physiological kneeling in healthy knees, and the
paper's own stated limitation is that knee flexion also includes squatting and lunging, which were
not studied. Abstract only, so nothing beyond the values above may be quoted. It cannot support any
claim about which direction to glide a knee, or about any other joint.

**Status of the rule itself: `Convention`.** Nothing reachable defines, derives or tests it. The
one clinical paper that engages it asks for it to be reviewed.

## Mulligan mobilization with movement

Two reachable meta-analyses. Both are positive; both have a design feature that decides how far
they can be read.

### Alqallaf A, et al. Mulligan Mobilization Combined with Conventional Therapy vs. Conventional Care Alone in Patients with Rotator Cuff Disease.
*J Clin Med* 2025;14(23):8352 · PMID 41375655 · PMC12692990 · **free full text** · PROSPERO CRD420251166854

Four RCTs, 160 participants; 80 received Mulligan plus conventional therapy (mean age 51 years,
mean 45% female). Random-effects, restricted maximum likelihood.

- **Supports — pain.** At rest, MD −1.19 (95% CI −1.64 to −0.74), **I² = 0%**. During activity,
  MD −2.25 (95% CI −3.18 to −1.31), I² = 67%.
- **Supports — function and motion.** Functionality MD −14.71 (95% CI −20.10 to −9.33), I² = 51%.
  Range of motion MD 19.92 (95% CI 11.25 to 28.39), I² = 58%. Joint position sense MD −3.31
  (95% CI −6.22 to −0.40), I² = 80%.
- **Supports — one honest null.** Quality of life MD 10.58 (95% CI −3.18 to 24.34), p = 0.13,
  I² = 76% — no significant difference.

**Does not support.** This is an **add-on design**: Mulligan plus conventional therapy against
conventional therapy alone, with no sham arm. It cannot separate the technique from the extra
contact time and attention that come with it — which is the whole question a student asks about
manual therapy. Four trials and 160 people. The abstract reports "ROM (MD 19.92)" without naming
the plane or the unit, so the guide may not render it as degrees of any named motion. And the
paper's background asserts that MWM works "by correcting positional faults": **that is the model's
claim, not a finding.** No positional fault was measured in any included trial.

### Shager K, Murugiah M, Sze Kei T, Ranganathan H, Ahmad MA. Effects of Mulligan sustained natural apophyseal glide mobilizations on pain, mobility, and lumbar-related disability in chronic non-specific low back pain.
*Medicine* 2026;105(26):e49456 · PMID 42363558 · PMC13313661 · **free full text**

Seventeen RCTs, 716 participants, published 2015–2024. Seven databases to June 2025. PEDro scores
4–9, mean 6.1; low-to-moderate risk of bias, chiefly allocation concealment and blinding.

- **Supports — pooled effects.** Pain intensity SMD −0.78 (P < .001), functional disability
  SMD −0.60 (P < .001), lumbar extension ROM SMD 0.75 (P < .001). Flexion ROM was **not**
  significant.
- **Supports — the authors' own certainty rating.** Moderate for pain; **low for disability and
  mobility**.

**Does not support.** The heterogeneity is close to total — **I² = 92% for pain, 83% for
disability, 93% for extension ROM**. A pooled estimate at that dispersion describes no single
population, and the guide must print the I² beside any SMD it quotes from this paper. Blinding was
one of the two main bias domains, so the comparison is largely unblinded manual contact against
less contact. Long-term follow-up was limited. This is the **lumbar spine**: it cannot be carried
across to hip, knee, ankle or shoulder mobilization, which is where this guide's other four
regions live.

## What mobilization does that can be measured

### Hadjisavvas S, Themistocleous IC, Efstathiou MA, Papamichael E, Michailidou C, Stefanakis M. The Effect of Joint Mobilization and Manipulation on Proprioception: Systematic Review with Limited Meta-Analysis.
*J Funct Morphol Kinesiol* 2026;11(1):59 · PMID 41718187 · PMC12921973 · **free full text**

483 records screened to seven RCTs (2018–2025), total n = 350, spinal or peripheral.

- **Supports — the individual trial results.** In chronic mechanical neck pain, cervical thrust
  manipulation improved joint position sense error versus sham with large effects
  (η²p = 0.23–0.36). Cervical mobilization improved left-rotation JPSE (4.15° → 1.65°, against
  4.01° → 3.74° in the comparator). In patellofemoral pain, lumbopelvic manipulation produced
  immediate reductions in knee JPSE at 60° (6.58° → 4.48°, against 5.91° → 6.05°).
- **Supports — how thin the evidence base is.** "No eligible randomized or sham-controlled trials
  meeting the prespecified criteria were identified before 2018."
- **Supports — the pooled result, which is null.** Only one outcome at one time-point could be
  meta-analysed (knee JPSE at 60° in patellofemoral pain, two trials): **Hedges' g = −0.21,
  95% CI −1.36 to 0.94, I² ≈ 83% — not significant.**

**Does not support.** Every effect above is **immediate post-intervention**; there is no follow-up.
Joint position sense error is a surrogate — no included trial linked it to pain or function. The
review pools mobilization and manipulation together as "articular manual therapy" and therefore
**cannot be cited for any distinction between them**. Seven trials, 350 people, and a single
poolable comparison that came out null.

### Jahromi MM, Basset D, Borrell JA, Sharma NK. Neurophysiological Response of Spinal Mobilization and Manipulation in Individuals With and Without Musculoskeletal Spine Disorders: A Scoping Review.
*Eur J Neurosci* 2026;64(2):e70632 · PMID 42478286 · PMC13386196 · **free full text**

Twenty-six studies across eight databases: 8 TMS, 7 fMRI, 5 somatosensory evoked potential, 6
other (EEG, PET, MRS).

- **Supports — that something measurable happens centrally.** Most studies reported significant
  post-intervention neurophysiological change, particularly in motor cortex excitability (TMS),
  functional connectivity (fMRI) and sensorimotor integration (SEP).
- **Supports — the sentence that matters most in this whole bank.** "Although many studies reported
  reductions in pain intensity, **none of them linked cortical changes directly to clinical
  improvements**."

**Does not support.** A scoping review does not appraise quality or pool results, so no effect size
exists here. It explicitly does not establish that the cortical change causes the pain relief — the
two were observed in the same studies and never connected. Heterogeneity in design, protocol,
outcome and timing is stated by the authors to limit generalizability. And like Hadjisavvas, it
treats manipulation and mobilization together.

### Cook CE, Keter D, Napadow V, Barbe MF, Perle SM, Bent J, Learman K, Reed WR. Manual therapy treatment mechanisms are complex: Challenges and a call to action.
*Complement Ther Med* 2026;100:103393 · PMID 42250858 · PMC13425405 · **free full text**

- **Supports — an argument, from named authors, about why the mechanism question stays open.** The
  authors argue that measuring the treatment mechanisms of force-based manipulation is highly
  complex because those mechanisms (1) frequently interact locally and regionally with unmeasured
  variables, (2) are influenced by time, clinician, environment "and a litany of other mediators",
  and (3) are likely variable because people respond differently to all interventions.

**Does not support.** **This is a commentary, not a study.** It contains no data, no participants
and no effect. It may be cited only for the position it takes, attributed as a commentary, and
never for any claim that mobilization does or does not work.

## Manipulation versus mobilization: the distinction is not resolved here

Three of the sources above bear on it and none settles it. Hadjisavvas pools the two as "articular
manual therapy." Jahromi reviews both and reports their neurophysiological responses together.
Cook writes about "force-based manipulations (which include manual therapies)" as one category.

**No reachable source separates them by outcome.** The guide states this as the finding it is,
rather than implying a difference the literature has not demonstrated — the same discipline the
other three banks applied to headline framing that outran its numbers.

## Cavitation — what the pop is, and what it is not

Two reachable studies. **They share a first author (Sillevis R) and the same portable-EEG method,
so they are one research line, not independent corroboration.** The guide must not present them as
two studies agreeing.

### Sillevis R, Selva-Sarzo F, Weiss V, Sanchez Romero EA. Do Audible Sounds During a Metacarpophalangeal and Metatarsophalangeal Thrust Manipulation Have an Impact on Intra-Articular Joint Space and Brainwave Activity?
*Healthcare (Basel)* 2025;13(5):554 · PMID 40077116 · PMC11898839 · **free full text**

Twenty-five healthy subjects, quasi-experimental repeated measures. Musculoskeletal ultrasound
measured intra-articular joint distance; a portable EEG captured brainwave activity across four
time-points.

- **Supports — the finding that settles the clinical question.** "**All subjects regardless of
  audible sound increased in joint space** following both the MCP and MTP joint manipulation." The
  joint separated whether or not it made a noise.
- **Supports — that the sound tracks something cortical, inconsistently.** The audible group had
  more significant EEG changes (p < 0.05) after MCP manipulation but **less** after MTP
  manipulation. MCP manipulation increased theta activity, which the authors read as a state of
  relaxation, larger in the audible group.
- **Supports — the authors' own verdict.** "Despite these findings, the clinical usefulness of
  audible sounds remains questionable."

**Does not support.** Twenty-five healthy subjects, second metacarpophalangeal and second
metatarsophalangeal joints — **not the hip, knee, shoulder or ankle this guide's other regions
cover**, and joint size is not incidental to a cavitation mechanism. No pain, function or symptom
outcome was measured at all. "A state of relaxation" is the authors' interpretation of a theta
increase, not a clinical finding. The two joints produced opposite EEG results and the paper offers
no explanation for that. The tribonucleation endorsement is an inference from joint-space change,
not direct imaging of a bubble.

### Sillevis R, Unum J, Weiss V, Shamus E, Selva-Sarzo F. The effect of a spinal thrust manipulation's audible pop on brain wave activity.
*PeerJ* 2024;12:e17622 · PMID 38952977 · PMC11216216 · **free full text**

Fifty-seven asymptomatic subjects underwent a prone T7 posterior-anterior thrust manipulation,
with 10 seconds of EEG recorded immediately afterwards (Emotiv EPOC+, 128 Hz, 14 electrodes) in an
environment controlled for electrical interference, temperature, lighting and sound.

- **Supports — both groups changed.** Non-audible group (n = 20): significant change under *some*
  electrodes in frontal, parietal and occipital lobes. Audible group (n = 37): significant change
  under *all* electrodes in frontal, parietal and occipital lobes, but **not** the temporal lobes.
- **Supports — the negative result the authors lead with.** The audible sound "did not affect the
  activity in the audible centers in the temporal brain region" — the pop is not simply being
  *heard*. The authors read the whole pattern as generalised relaxation following thrust
  manipulation with or without a sound.

**Does not support.** Asymptomatic volunteers, no clinical outcome, ten seconds of recording.
**Group allocation was by whether a pop happened**, so the groups are self-selected by how each
person's joint responded — this is not a randomised comparison and cannot support a causal claim
about the sound. The authors' suggestion that the frontal-lobe result "might indicate that the
audible pop does not produce a 'placebo' mechanism" is explicitly speculative and must not be
quoted as a finding about placebo.

**What the guide can say:** the joint gapped whether or not it popped, and the pop did not light up
auditory cortex. Whatever the sound is, no reachable source ties it to a better outcome.

## Harms, contraindications and screening

### Daniels CJ, Farabaugh RJ, Salsbury SA, Anderson KR, Kleppe MJ, Whalen WM, Walters SA, Killinger LZ, Schielke AL. Adverse events among older adults receiving chiropractic spinal manipulation and related treatments: an updated systematic review.
*Chiropr Man Therap* 2026;34(1):15 · PMID 41840685 · PMC13104207 · **free full text** · PROSPERO CRD42024629286

Adults over 55. Five databases searched 12 December 2025, search strategy audited by PRESS. 2295
titles screened, 125 full texts, 25 articles included: 6 RCTs, 4 observational studies, 15 case
reports or series. One RCT at low risk of bias and five acceptable; three observational cohorts low
and one acceptable.

- **Supports — a real harms profile.** **412 study-related adverse events**, of which **9 were
  severe** and **none catastrophic**. Most were mild-to-moderate: changes in pain quality, muscle
  or joint soreness or stiffness, numbness, weakness, fatigue, headache, dizziness or
  lightheadedness.
- **Supports — where the severe events sit.** The case reports described 6 severe adverse events,
  **four of them vascular**. Twenty of the case reports involved spinal manipulation, one followed
  TENS and one repetitive shoulder abduction; **no case report described an adverse event following
  soft tissue techniques, exercise, or other modalities.**
- **Supports — the ascertainment problem, stated by the authors.** "Several studies lacked AE
  definitions and/or data collection processes."

**Does not support — and this is the load-bearing limitation.** This review **cannot establish that
catastrophic events are rare**, because it excluded administrative database studies, and the
authors name that as a limitation precisely because those are the studies "that may detect rare
AEs." *No catastrophic adverse events were reported* is not *catastrophic adverse events do not
occur*; the design could not have found them. The search was also focused on spinal manipulation,
so the figures do not transfer to peripheral joint mobilization, and the population is over 55
only. The 412 count is a floor produced by inconsistent ascertainment, not an incidence — there is
no denominator here. **Funding: partially funded by Clinical Compass and the NCMIC Foundation**,
both chiropractic organisations; recorded because a harms review funded by the profession under
review is a conflict a reader should weigh.

### Searched, not found — a validated pre-manipulative screening test

Nothing reachable validates a pre-treatment screening procedure against the outcome it is meant to
prevent. The one adjacent hit was a **survey of chiropractic faculty** on how they teach the
relationship between cervical adjusting and cervical artery dissection (*Chiropr Man Therap* 2026;
abstract only) — a study of what is taught, not of whether screening works.

## Capsular pattern and packed positions

### Capsular pattern — already answered, in another bank

The hip build resolved this and the finding transfers directly. Bijl D, et al., *Validity of
Cyriax's concept capsular pattern for the diagnosis of osteoarthritis of hip and/or knee*, *Scand J
Rheumatol* 1998;27(5):347-351: in 200 patients the pattern Cyriax describes for the hip was "not
present as a distinct pattern", and the capsular pattern "cannot be regarded as a valid test for
the diagnosis of OA of the hip or knee." See `docs/hip-guide-build/sources.md`. **The guide cites
Bijl rather than re-deriving it.**

### Searched, not found — open-packed and close-packed positions

`"close-packed position"`, `"closed-packed position"`, `"open-packed position"` and
`"loose-packed position"` return nothing that defines, derives or tests these positions. Results
were shoulder instability surgery, primate wrist morphology, muscle energy technique and a squat
commentary — the terms appear as assumed vocabulary, never as the object of study.

**Status: `Convention`.** Every joint's open-packed position is tabulated identically in every
textbook, and the position a clinician mobilises in is chosen by it. Nothing reachable says where
the numbers came from. This is the same shape as the grading systems, and it is the second
load-bearing convention this build has failed to trace.

## A conflation to avoid

Searching guideline text for `grade III` returns **Kellgren-Lawrence Grade III hip osteoarthritis**
as readily as anything about mobilization. Two unrelated scales share the word, and one of them is
radiographic severity. **Every "grade" in this guide must name its scale.** This is the same class
of near-miss as the talar tilt search-strategy hit recorded in the ankle bank.

## Source bank: complete

Every item on the original chase list has been run to a determination — a source, or a recorded
"searched, not found". Nothing is left open. Three findings are load-bearing for the draft:

1. **The grading systems have no reachable origin, definition or reliability data** (above).
2. **Open-packed and close-packed positions have no reachable basis either** (above) — so two of
   the conventions that decide how a mobilization is performed are untraceable, not one.
3. **What *is* measured** — force relative to tissue tolerance (hip guideline), where you stop
   relative to resistance, inter-rater agreement on an accessory glide (Nguyen, ICC 0.64), and a
   central response that nobody has linked to clinical improvement (Jahromi).

Status: **ready to draft.**
