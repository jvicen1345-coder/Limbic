#ItemWhat it coversThe point to know
Before any manual muscle test
1 Active range first Watch the movement actively through its range before you resist it, and measure the range you get. A muscle can only be graded through the range the joint has. Record a grade through a limited range as such (for example, '4 through 0–90°') — grading a stiff joint as weak is the commonest error in the whole examination. Convention
2 Know the 0–5 scale Learn the six grades by what the muscle has to do, not by adjectives: contraction, range without gravity, range against gravity, then resistance. 0 no contraction; 1 palpable or visible contraction; 2 full range with gravity eliminated; 3 full range against gravity; 4 full range against moderate resistance; 5 full range against full resistance Rupp 2021. Plus and minus modifiers (3+, 4−) are added by convention and are applied inconsistently between texts. Convention
3 Against gravity decides the path Test in the against-gravity position first. If the patient cannot complete the range, reposition gravity-minimized; if they can, add resistance. Grade 3 is the branch point: below it you test without gravity, above it you test against your hand. Positioning every patient gravity-minimized first wastes the visit. Convention
4 Stabilize proximally Fix the segment the muscle arises from with your other hand or the table, so the tested segment moves and nothing else does. Without stabilization the strongest muscle nearby does the test — the pelvis tilts in a hip test, the scapula moves in a shoulder test. The finding is movement of the stabilized segment. Convention
5 Break test Patient holds the end position; you build pressure gradually over about two seconds and try to move the segment out of it. A held position that yields is a 4; one that does not is a 5. A make test (patient pushes against your fixed hand) is the alternative; the two give different forces and should not be mixed in one record. Convention
6 Resistance where it counts Resist at the distal end of the segment the muscle moves, perpendicular to it, never across a second joint. A long lever makes your hand stronger than the patient; resistance across two joints tests two muscle groups. Record where you resisted if you deviate from the standard point. Convention
7 Watch for substitution Name, before you test, the muscle most likely to fake the movement — the last column of every table — and watch for it. The substitution changes the direction of the movement, not just its quality: shoulder abduction that drifts into flexion, hip flexion that rolls out. See the 'What fakes it' column. Convention
8 Compare sides and record pain Test the uninvolved side first, then the involved side, and note pain with each contraction. Weak and painful is not weak — pain inhibits force and a painful muscle cannot be graded as if it were a nerve problem. Record 'strong and painful', 'weak and painless' and so on. Convention
9 Know the ceiling When the difference between grade 4 and 5 matters — return to sport, serial measurement — use a hand-held dynamometer instead. Grades 4 and 5 cover most of a muscle's force range, and a hand cannot separate them reliably; a strong patient beats an examiner at the hip, knee and ankle. Convention
Upper quarter — know each group cold
10 Scapular stabilizers Trapezius in three parts, serratus anterior, rhomboids, levator scapulae, pectoralis minor. Upward rotation is a three-muscle force couple (upper and lower trapezius, serratus anterior). Serratus loss wings medially; trapezius loss wings laterally.
11 Deltoid and the rotator cuff Three parts of deltoid; supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, subscapularis. Deltoid moves the arm; the cuff holds the head down while it does. Axillary nerve: deltoid and teres minor. Suprascapular: supraspinatus and infraspinatus.
12 The three adductor–medial rotators Latissimus dorsi, teres major, pectoralis major — the intertubercular groove. Lady between two majors: latissimus in the floor, pectoralis major on the lateral lip, teres major on the medial lip.
13 Elbow flexors and extensors Biceps, brachialis, brachioradialis; triceps and anconeus. Forearm position picks the flexor: supinated biceps, neutral brachioradialis, any position brachialis. Elbow flexors are C5, extensors C7 Rupp 2021.
14 Forearm rotators Supinator and biceps; pronator teres and pronator quadratus. Biceps is the strongest supinator at 90° of elbow flexion; pronator quadratus pronates in any elbow position.
15 Wrist flexors and extensors FCR, palmaris longus, FCU from the medial epicondyle; ECRL, ECRB, ECU from the lateral side. Keep the fingers relaxed or the finger muscles move the wrist. Wrist extensors are the C6 key muscle Rupp 2021.
16 Long finger and thumb muscles FDS, FDP, FPL; extensor digitorum, indicis, digiti minimi; APL, EPB, EPL. FDS flexes the PIP, FDP the DIP. FDP to the middle finger is the C8 key muscle Rupp 2021. Tenodesis fakes every one of these tests.
17 Hand intrinsics Thenar, hypothenar, adductor pollicis, lumbricals, dorsal and palmar interossei. LOAF are median; everything else is ulnar. PAD and DAB about the middle finger. Little finger abduction is the T1 key muscle Rupp 2021.
Lower quarter — know each group cold
18 Hip flexors Iliopsoas, with rectus femoris, sartorius, TFL and pectineus assisting. Hip flexors are the L2 key muscle Rupp 2021. Thigh rolling out is sartorius; rolling in is TFL.
19 Gluteals and TFL Gluteus maximus, medius, minimus; tensor fasciae latae. Knee flexed takes the hamstrings out of the gluteus maximus test. Superior gluteal nerve loss gives the Trendelenburg sign; inferior gluteal loss, trouble rising and climbing.
20 Deep six lateral rotators Piriformis, superior gemellus, obturator internus, inferior gemellus, quadratus femoris, obturator externus. Tested only as a group, in sitting. Obturator externus is the one on the obturator nerve.
21 Quadriceps and hamstrings Four quadriceps heads; semitendinosus, semimembranosus, biceps femoris long and short heads. Knee extensors are the L3 key muscle Rupp 2021. Short head of biceps is the one hamstring on the common fibular division.
22 Adductors and the pes anserinus Adductor longus, brevis, magnus, pectineus, gracilis; sartorius, gracilis and semitendinosus at the pes. Pes anserinus: three muscles, three compartments, three nerves (femoral, obturator, tibial). Adductor magnus has two nerves.
23 Anterior and lateral leg Tibialis anterior, EHL, EDL, fibularis tertius; fibularis longus and brevis. Dorsiflexors are L4, long toe extensors L5 Rupp 2021. Foot drop with eversion spared is deep fibular; with eversion weak, common fibular.
24 Posterior leg Gastrocnemius, soleus, plantaris, popliteus, tibialis posterior, FDL, FHL. Plantarflexors are S1 Rupp 2021, and are tested standing because a hand cannot break them.
25 Foot intrinsics Two dorsal muscles (EDB, EHB); four plantar layers on the medial and lateral plantar nerves. Most are observed, not graded. EDB wasting is an early deep fibular or L5 sign.
Trunk, neck and head
26 Abdominals Rectus abdominis, external and internal obliques, transversus abdominis, pyramidalis. Grade by arm position, not by your hand. Rotation to one side uses the opposite external and the same-side internal oblique.
27 Back and quadratus lumborum Erector spinae (I Love Spaghetti), transversospinalis, quadratus lumborum. Stabilize the legs or gluteus maximus lifts the trunk. Quadratus lumborum hikes the pelvis against traction.
28 Neck flexors and extensors SCM, scalenes, longus colli and capitis, splenius, semispinalis, suboccipitals. A strong head lift says nothing about the deep flexors — the craniocervical flexion test does. SCM turns the face to the opposite side.
29 Breathing and pelvic floor Diaphragm, intercostals, accessory muscles; levator ani, coccygeus, sphincters. Diaphragm is C3–C5. Voluntary anal contraction is part of the spinal cord injury examination Rupp 2021.
30 Mastication and the face Masseter, temporalis, pterygoids (CN V3); muscles of facial expression (CN VII). Jaw deviates toward the weak lateral pterygoid. A forehead that moves means an upper motor neuron facial palsy; a still forehead means lower.
31 Eye, tongue, palate and larynx Extraocular muscles (III, IV, VI), tongue (XII), palate and pharynx (IX, X), larynx (X). LR6 SO4, the rest 3. Tongue deviates toward the weak side; the uvula away from it.
Putting it together
32 Myotome screen Test the ten ISNCSCI key muscle functions per side, supine, as a screen before regional testing. C5 elbow flexors, C6 wrist extensors, C7 elbow extensors, C8 middle finger DIP flexion, T1 little finger abduction; L2 hip flexors, L3 knee extensors, L4 ankle dorsiflexors, L5 long toe extensors, S1 ankle plantar flexors Rupp 2021.

A root lesion crosses nerve boundaries; a nerve lesion crosses root boundaries. Foot drop with weak inversion (tibialis posterior, L5 via the tibial nerve) is L5; with inversion intact it is the fibular nerve. See the Nerves table.

03

How a manual muscle test is graded

The international spinal cord injury standard defines six motor grades by what the muscle has to do, and every test in this guide is graded on them. The finer steps between them (3+, 4−) are added by convention and used differently in different texts, so record what the patient did alongside the number.

GradeDefinitionWhat earns it
0 No contraction. Total paralysis — nothing visible or palpable over the muscle or its tendon Rupp 2021.
1 Contraction without movement. A palpable or visible contraction — feel the tendon or belly while the patient tries Rupp 2021.
2 Full range, gravity eliminated. Active movement through the full range with gravity eliminated — the gravity-minimized position in each muscle row Rupp 2021. A 2− moves through part of the range. Convention
3 Full range against gravity. Active movement through the full range against gravity, with no added resistance Rupp 2021. A 3− gets more than half the range against gravity. Convention
4 Full range against moderate resistance. Holds against moderate resistance in the muscle-specific position Rupp 2021, then yields to a break test. Convention
5 Full range against full resistance. Normal: full resistance expected from an otherwise unimpaired person Rupp 2021. Judged against the other side and the patient's build, age and sex — it is a comparison, not an absolute force.
NT Not testable. Record NT when pain, immobilization or a contracture prevents testing, rather than guessing a grade Rupp 2021.
Make vs break Two ways to apply resistance. Break test: patient holds, examiner pushes until it gives. Make test: patient pushes against a fixed hand. They produce different forces; use one method consistently. Convention
Gravity-minimized Moving in the horizontal plane. The limb moves parallel to the floor, supported on a table or in sidelying, so gravity neither helps nor resists. Each muscle row gives the position. Convention
Screening versus grading Two different jobs. A strength screen asks 'normal or not' at the ten key muscles; grading asks 'how weak' of the muscles the screen implicated. The review of grading systems is Naqvi 2025.
A weak and painful muscle has not been graded. Pain inhibits force, so a painful contraction produces a low grade whatever the muscle's real strength. Record the pain and the grade together, and do not read a painful 4 as a nerve or root finding. Convention
Most substitutions change the direction, not the effort. The substituting muscle moves the segment along its own line of pull, so the movement drifts — abduction into flexion, hip flexion into lateral rotation. Watch the direction and the stabilized segment; the last column of every muscle table names what to watch for. Convention
04

Shoulder girdle — the muscles that place the scapula

Every arm movement starts here: these muscles position and hold the scapula so the glenohumeral muscles have a stable base to pull from. Trapezius is tested as three muscles because its three fibre groups pull in three directions and fail separately. The test position is the finding's context: watch the scapula, not the hand, because nearly every substitution in this region moves the arm correctly while the scapula does something else.

Posterior — trapezius, levator scapulae, rhomboids
Upper trapezius Ourieff 2023 External occipital protuberance, medial third of the superior nuchal line, ligamentum nuchae, spinous process of C7. Posterior border of the lateral third of the clavicle and the acromion. Spinal accessory n. (CN XI), motor; C3–C4 carry proprioception. Elevates the scapula and upwardly rotates it with lower trapezius and serratus anterior; extends and laterally flexes the neck to the same side, rotates the face to the opposite side. Position: sitting, arms at the sides. Stabilize: none needed — test both sides together. Test: patient shrugs both shoulders toward the ears; resist straight down on top of both shoulders. Grade 2: same, with a smaller range, or sitting with arms supported. Convention Levator scapulae and rhomboids also shrug — they elevate with the inferior angle swinging medially (downward rotation), where upper trapezius swings it laterally. Neck side-bending toward the shoulder shortens the distance without elevating the scapula.
Middle trapezius Ourieff 2023 Spinous processes and supraspinous ligament of T1–T5. Medial margin of the acromion and superior lip of the spine of the scapula. Spinal accessory n. (CN XI). Retracts (adducts) the scapula. Position: prone, shoulder abducted 90° and laterally rotated (thumb up), elbow extended. Stabilize: the opposite thorax. Test: patient lifts the arm toward the ceiling, drawing the scapula toward the spine; resist downward at the distal arm or over the lateral scapula. Grade 2: sitting, arm supported on a table at 90°, retract across the surface. Convention Posterior deltoid lifts the arm while the scapula stays put — the arm rises, the medial border does not move toward the spine. Rhomboids retract with downward rotation (inferior angle swings in); trunk rotation fakes range.
Lower trapezius Ourieff 2023 Spinous processes of T6–T12. Tubercle at the medial end (root) of the spine of the scapula. Spinal accessory n. (CN XI). Depresses the scapula and upwardly rotates it; assists retraction. Position: prone, arm overhead at about 145° of abduction in line with the lower fibres, thumb up. Stabilize: the opposite thorax. Test: patient lifts the arm from the table; resist downward at the distal arm. If shoulder range or deltoid weakness prevents the arm position, resist at the lateral scapula instead. Grade 2: same position, observe and palpate depression and retraction of the scapula without the arm lifted. Convention The arm lifts with the scapula elevating — upper trapezius or levator doing the work. Lumbar extension raises the whole trunk and looks like range.
Levator scapulae Henry 2023 Posterior tubercles of the transverse processes of C1–C4. Medial border of the scapula from the superior angle to the root of the spine. Dorsal scapular n. (C5) and cervical nerves C3–C4. Elevates the scapula and tilts the glenoid inferiorly (downward rotation); laterally flexes the neck. No isolated manual test. It works in the shrug with upper trapezius; tell them apart by the inferior angle, which swings medially when levator dominates. Palpate deep to upper trapezius just above the superior angle. Convention Upper trapezius hides it completely in the shrug. A shrug that drops the glenoid and brings the inferior angle toward the spine is levator- and rhomboid-dominant.
Rhomboid major Farrell 2023 Spinous processes of T2–T5. Medial border of the scapula from the root of the spine to the inferior angle. Dorsal scapular n. (C4, C5). Retracts and downwardly rotates the scapula; fixes it to the thoracic wall. Position: prone, hand resting on the low back (shoulder adducted, extended and internally rotated, elbow flexed). Stabilize: the opposite thorax. Test: patient lifts the hand off the back, keeping the arm in place; resist at the distal arm in the direction of scapular abduction and upward rotation (out and down). Grade 2: sitting, hand behind the back; observe the medial border move toward the spine. Convention Posterior deltoid and teres major lift the hand without the scapula moving. Middle trapezius retracts without downward rotation, so watch the inferior angle. Anterior tilt from pectoralis minor lifts the hand by tipping the scapula.
Rhomboid minor Farrell 2023 Ligamentum nuchae and spinous processes of C7–T1. Medial border of the scapula at the root of the spine. Dorsal scapular n. (C4, C5). Retracts and downwardly rotates the scapula; fixes it to the thoracic wall. Tested with rhomboid major — same position, same resistance. Convention Same as rhomboid major.
Anterior — serratus anterior, pectoralis minor, subclavius
Serratus anterior St Lucia 2024 External surfaces of the lateral parts of ribs 1–8 (often 9). Anterior (costal) surface of the medial border of the scapula, most heavily at the inferior angle. Long thoracic n. (C5, C6, C7). Protracts the scapula and holds it against the thoracic wall; upwardly rotates it (inferior angle forward and lateral). Position: sitting, shoulder flexed to about 130°, elbow extended. Stabilize: the thorax, with a hand ready at the inferior angle. Test: resist downward at the distal arm while palpating the inferior angle; the scapula should hold its upwardly rotated position against the chest wall. Supine alternative: shoulder flexed 90°, patient punches toward the ceiling; resist straight down through the fist. Grade 2: sitting, arm supported on a table at 90°, slide the arm forward; observe protraction. Convention Winging is the finding — the medial border and inferior angle lift off the chest wall. Pectoralis major pushes the arm forward across the body (horizontal adduction) instead of protracting the scapula; elbow flexion in the supine punch lets triceps and pectoralis do the work.
Pectoralis minor Baig 2023 Ribs 3–5 near their costal cartilages. Medial border and superior surface of the coracoid process. Medial pectoral n. (C8, T1). Draws the scapula forward and down against the thorax (depression, anterior tilt, downward rotation); raises ribs 3–5 in forced inspiration when the scapula is fixed. Position: supine, arm at the side. Stabilize: the opposite side of the thorax. Test: patient lifts the front of the shoulder up off the table without pushing through the hand; resist at the anterior shoulder back toward the table. Grade 2: same, gravity-minimized is not practical — observe and palpate below the coracoid. Convention Pushing through the hand or elbow recruits triceps and latissimus to lift the shoulder. Shoulder elevation (shrugging) substitutes for anterior tilt.
Subclavius Tang 2023 Junction of rib 1 and its costal cartilage. Inferior surface of the middle third of the clavicle. Nerve to subclavius (C5, C6). Anchors and depresses the clavicle. No clinical test. It cannot be isolated or palpated through pectoralis major. Convention Nothing to fake — there is no test to fake.
05

Glenohumeral — the deltoid, the cuff, and the three big adductors

Deltoid moves the arm; the rotator cuff keeps the humeral head on the glenoid while it does. The three large adductor–internal rotators (latissimus, teres major, pectoralis major) share a job and a groove on the humerus. Deltoid is tested as three muscles, as trapezius is, because its three parts have three different lines of pull.

Anterior deltoid Elzanie 2024 Anterior border and superior surface of the lateral third of the clavicle. Deltoid tuberosity of the humerus. Axillary n. (C5, C6). Flexes and medially rotates the arm. Abducts the arm beyond the first ~15°, which supraspinatus initiates. Position: sitting, shoulder abducted to 90°, elbow slightly flexed, palm down. Stabilize: over the superior scapula. Posterior deltoid Elzanie 2024 Inferior lip of the spine of the scapula. Deltoid tuberosity of the humerus. Axillary n. (C5, C6). Extends and laterally rotates the arm; horizontally abducts. Position: prone, shoulder abducted 90° with the arm over the table edge, elbow flexed 90° hanging. Stabilize: over the scapula. Test: patient lifts the elbow toward the ceiling (horizontal abduction); resist downward at the distal humerus. Grade 2: sitting, arm supported at 90° on a table, slide it backward. Convention Rhomboids and middle trapezius retract the scapula, carrying the arm back with no glenohumeral motion. Long head of triceps helps if the elbow is extended.
Rotator cuff — supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, subscapularis
Supraspinatus Munjal 2023 Supraspinous fossa of the scapula. Superior facet of the greater tubercle of the humerus. Suprascapular n. (C5, C6). Initiates abduction and works with deltoid through the range; compresses the humeral head into the glenoid. Position: sitting or standing, arm elevated to 90° in the scapular plane (about 30° forward of the frontal plane), elbow extended, thumb down (empty can) or thumb up at 45° lateral rotation (full can). Stabilize: over the superior scapula. Test: hold; resist downward at the distal forearm or arm. Convention Pain inhibition reads as weakness — a painful, weak result does not separate a tear from a painful tendon. Upper trapezius hikes the shoulder; deltoid is loaded in the same position, so the test biases supraspinatus rather than isolating it.
Infraspinatus Williams 2023 Infraspinous fossa of the scapula. Middle facet of the greater tubercle of the humerus. Suprascapular n. (C5, C6). Laterally rotates the arm; holds the humeral head in the glenoid. Position: prone, shoulder abducted 90° with the arm on the table, elbow flexed 90° over the edge, forearm hanging. Stabilize: the distal humerus on the table. Test: patient rotates the forearm up toward the ceiling (lateral rotation); resist at the distal forearm toward medial rotation. Sitting alternative: arm at the side, elbow at 90°, resist rotation outward. Grade 2: prone with the whole limb hanging over the edge, rotate the arm outward. Convention Elbow extension and supination swing the forearm through the arc without rotating the humerus. Scapular retraction and trunk rotation fake range in sitting.
Teres minor La Rose 2026 Middle part of the lateral border of the scapula. Inferior facet of the greater tubercle of the humerus. Axillary n. (C5, C6). Laterally rotates the arm; holds the humeral head in the glenoid. Tested with infraspinatus. Lateral rotation resisted with the arm abducted 90° in the scapular plane (hornblower position) biases teres minor. Convention Same as infraspinatus.
Subscapularis Aguirre 2023 Subscapular fossa (costal surface of the scapula). Lesser tubercle of the humerus. Upper and lower subscapular nn. (C5, C6, C7). Medially rotates the arm; holds the humeral head in the glenoid. Position: prone, shoulder abducted 90°, elbow flexed 90° over the table edge. Stabilize: the distal humerus on the table. Test: patient moves the palm up and back toward the ceiling (medial rotation); resist at the distal forearm toward lateral rotation. Lift-off: hand behind the back at waist level, lift it away from the back. Belly press: press the palm into the abdomen with the elbow held forward. Grade 2: prone with the limb hanging, rotate the arm inward. Convention Pectoralis major, latissimus and teres major all rotate medially — the standard test cannot isolate subscapularis; lift-off and belly press bias it. Elbow extension or scapular anterior tilt lifts the hand in the lift-off without rotation.
Adductor–medial rotators — latissimus dorsi, teres major, pectoralis major, coracobrachialis
Latissimus dorsi Jeno 2023 Spinous processes of T7–T12, thoracolumbar fascia, iliac crest, inferior 3–4 ribs. Floor of the intertubercular sulcus of the humerus. Thoracodorsal n. (C6, C7, C8). Extends, adducts and medially rotates the humerus; raises the body toward the arms in climbing; depresses the shoulder girdle. Position: prone, arm at the side, medially rotated (palm up). Stabilize: the pelvis on the same side. Test: patient lifts the arm toward the ceiling (extension with adduction); resist at the distal forearm toward flexion and abduction. Functional alternative: sitting, hands on the table, press down to lift the buttocks (shoulder girdle depression). Grade 2: sidelying, extend the arm along the table. Convention Posterior deltoid and teres major extend the arm too; the depression press-up needs triceps unless the elbows are kept straight and locked. Trunk side-bending lifts the arm through the pelvis.
Teres major Syros 2023 Posterior surface of the inferior angle of the scapula. Medial lip of the intertubercular sulcus (crest of the lesser tubercle). Lower subscapular n. (C5, C6, C7). Adducts, extends and medially rotates the arm. Position: prone, hand resting on the posterior iliac crest (shoulder extended, adducted, medially rotated, elbow flexed). Stabilize: the opposite thorax. Test: patient lifts the elbow toward the ceiling; resist at the distal arm toward abduction and flexion. Convention Rhomboids lift the elbow by retracting the scapula — the arm rises without glenohumeral extension. Latissimus contributes in the same position.
Pectoralis major — clavicular head Solari 2023 Anterior surface of the medial half of the clavicle. Lateral lip of the intertubercular sulcus of the humerus. Lateral pectoral n. (C5, C6, C7). Adducts and medially rotates the arm; flexes the arm from extension; horizontally adducts. Position: supine, shoulder flexed to 90° (or abducted 90° with elbow flexed), slight medial rotation. Stabilize: the opposite shoulder. Test: patient moves the arm up and across toward the opposite shoulder; resist at the distal arm toward horizontal abduction. Grade 2: sitting, arm supported at 90° on a table, slide across the body. Convention Trunk rotation and shoulder protraction carry the arm across. Anterior deltoid helps in the flexed start; a clavicular test that fails while the sternocostal one passes localizes to the lateral pectoral nerve.
Pectoralis major — sternocostal head Solari 2023 Anterior sternum, costal cartilages 1–6, aponeurosis of external oblique. Lateral lip of the intertubercular sulcus of the humerus. Lateral and medial pectoral nn. (C7, C8, T1). Adducts and medially rotates the arm; extends the arm from flexion. Position: supine, shoulder flexed to about 120° and slightly abducted. Stabilize: the opposite iliac crest. Test: patient pulls the arm down and across toward the opposite hip; resist at the distal arm upward and outward. Grade 2: sitting, arm supported on a table, slide it down and across. Convention Latissimus pulls the arm down without the across component. Trunk flexion and rotation fake the arc.
Coracobrachialis Alshammari 2023 Tip of the coracoid process. Medial surface of the middle third of the humerus. Musculocutaneous n. (C5, C6, C7). Flexes and adducts the arm; helps hold the humeral head in the glenoid. Position: sitting, shoulder laterally rotated and flexed, elbow fully flexed, forearm supinated — full elbow flexion leaves biceps too short to contribute. Stabilize: the shoulder girdle. Test: resist at the anteromedial distal arm toward extension and slight abduction. Convention Biceps takes over if the elbow is allowed to extend, and anterior deltoid always contributes to flexion. Elbow and forearm — flexors, extensors and the forearm rotators

Three elbow flexors, and forearm position decides which one is doing the most: biceps with the forearm supinated, brachialis in every position, brachioradialis in mid-position. That is the whole test design — change the forearm, change the muscle. The rotators work the same way: biceps is the strongest supinator with the elbow at 90°, and elbow position is how the tests shift load between the pronators.

07

Wrist and extrinsic finger muscles

The flexors come off the medial epicondyle, the extensors off the lateral epicondyle, and the long finger and thumb muscles cross the wrist on their way to the digits. That last fact is the source of most errors here: a wrist test with the fingers working is partly a finger test, and a finger test with the wrist moving is partly tenodesis. Keep the joint you are not testing still.

Almost every intrinsic muscle is on the ulnar nerve's deep branch; the exceptions — the thenar trio and the lateral two lumbricals — are the median nerve's (the mnemonic is LOAF: lumbricals 1–2, opponens, abductor, flexor brevis). Hip — flexors, gluteals, and the deep lateral rotators

Iliopsoas flexes, the gluteals extend and abduct, and six small muscles behind the hip rotate it laterally and hold the femoral head in the acetabulum. Most hip tests fail the same way: the pelvis and lumbar spine move instead of the femur. Stabilize the pelvis, and watch it — a hip test graded while the pelvis tilts is a trunk test.

Thigh — quadriceps, hamstrings and adductors

Three compartments, three nerves: anterior on the femoral, medial on the obturator, posterior on the sciatic. The exceptions are the ones examiners ask about — pectineus (femoral, sometimes obturator too), the hamstring part of adductor magnus (tibial), and the short head of biceps femoris (common fibular). The two-joint muscles are the testing problem: rectus femoris and the hamstrings each act at the hip and the knee, and joint position is how you make them work or keep them out.

Trunk — abdominal wall, quadratus lumborum and the back

Trunk strength is graded by lever length, not by the examiner's hand: the arm position changes how much of the body the muscles have to lift. The abdominal wall is four muscles in three layers whose fibres cross at angles, and the back is a superficial column (erector spinae) over a deep one (transversospinalis). All the back muscles proper are on the posterior rami — the ones on the back that are not (trapezius, latissimus, rhomboids, serratus posterior) moved there from the limb or the ribs.

Breathing muscles and the pelvic floor

The diaphragm does quiet inspiration by itself; everything else here is recruited when breathing gets harder or when the abdomen has to hold pressure. The pelvic floor closes the bottom of the same pressurized canister. None of these is graded against the examiner's hand — they are observed, measured, or, for the pelvic floor, palpated internally by a clinician trained and consented to do it.

Neck — flexors, extensors, the hyoid muscles and platysma

Two neck flexor systems: superficial (SCM and scalenes), which lift the head by pushing the chin forward, and deep (longus colli and capitis), which nod the chin down and hold the cervical curve. A strong head lift says nothing about the deep flexors — that is the reason the craniocervical flexion test exists. The hyoid muscles act on the jaw and the larynx rather than the neck, and are tested in swallowing.

Head — chewing, the face, the eye, and the throat

Almost nothing above the neck is graded 0–5 against resistance. The face is graded by what it can do — normal, weak, trace, absent — and the throat and eye by whether a movement happens and which way it deviates. The deviation is the finding, and each nerve has its own rule: the jaw and the tongue deviate toward the weak side, the uvula away from it. Muscles that work together — named groups and force couples

the relationship is the answer

A muscle is rarely asked about alone. Examiners and clinicians talk in groups — the rotator cuff, the pes anserinus, the deep six — because the group shares an attachment, a nerve, a compartment, or a job, and that shared thing is what the question is really about. A force couple is two or more muscles pulling in different directions that together produce one rotation; lose one member and the rotation fails in a characteristic way, which is why the last column here is what the failure looks like.

Shoulder girdle and shoulder
Scapular upward rotation force couple Upper trapezius, lower trapezius, serratus anterior. Ourieff 2023 St Lucia 2024 Three lines of pull on the scapula — upper trapezius up and in at the acromion, lower trapezius down and in at the root of the spine, serratus anterior forward and out at the inferior angle — add up to one rotation that turns the glenoid upward as the arm rises. See the figure below. Arm elevation stops short or the scapula wings. Serratus loss: the medial border and inferior angle lift off the ribs (medial winging), worst pushing against a wall. Trapezius loss: the shoulder droops and the scapula slides laterally with the inferior angle rotated out (lateral winging), worst in abduction.

Anterior and posterior axillary folds Pectoralis major (anterior fold); latissimus dorsi and teres major (posterior fold). The two walls of the armpit you can grip. Elbow, forearm and hand
Elbow flexor trio Biceps brachii, brachialis, brachioradialis. Alshammari 2023 All flex the elbow; forearm rotation picks which does most. Supinated favours biceps, mid-position favours brachioradialis, and brachialis works in all three. Nerve localization: biceps and brachialis on the musculocutaneous, brachioradialis on the radial. Weak flexion supinated with brachioradialis intact is musculocutaneous; the reverse is radial.
Forearm rotators Supinators: supinator, biceps. Pronators: pronator teres, pronator quadratus. Opposing pairs acting on the radius. Biceps adds power to supination with the elbow flexed; pronator quadratus is the one that works regardless of elbow position. Supination strength drops with the elbow extended in a biceps rupture (the supinator is left alone); both pronators are on the median nerve, so a median lesion above the elbow leaves only brachioradialis, which can bring a supinated forearm back to neutral and no further.
Common flexor origin (medial epicondyle) Pronator teres, flexor carpi radialis, palmaris longus, flexor carpi ulnaris, flexor digitorum superficialis. Share one tendon origin on the medial epicondyle. Medial epicondyle pain on resisted wrist flexion and pronation — the pattern of medial epicondylalgia. Convention
Common extensor origin (lateral epicondyle) Extensor carpi radialis brevis, extensor digitorum, extensor digiti minimi, extensor carpi ulnaris (and supinator, anconeus nearby). Share one tendon origin on the lateral epicondyle; ECRB's part is deepest. Lateral epicondyle pain on resisted wrist or middle finger extension, and on gripping — the pattern of lateral epicondylalgia. Convention
Grip synergy — finger flexors with wrist extensors FDS and FDP (grip) with ECRB, ECRL and ECU (wrist hold). The finger flexors also flex the wrist; the wrist extensors must contract to stop them, holding the wrist in slight extension where the flexors are at a strong length. Grip is weak and painful when the wrist extensors are — and a flexed wrist makes any grip weaker, which is why wrist position changes a grip dynamometer reading.
Tenodesis Long finger flexors and extensors, with active wrist extension. Extending the wrist stretches the finger flexors, which curls the fingers; flexing the wrist opens them. No finger muscle has to contract. The functional grip of C6 tetraplegia: active wrist extension closes the hand. The same mechanism fakes finger flexion on a manual muscle test — keep the wrist still.
Anatomical snuffbox Abductor pollicis longus and extensor pollicis brevis (radial border); extensor pollicis longus (ulnar border). Hallett Reid 2025 The hollow between their tendons when the thumb extends; the scaphoid is its floor and the radial artery crosses it. Thenar eminence Abductor pollicis brevis, flexor pollicis brevis, opponens pollicis (with adductor pollicis deep to them). Luxenburg 2023 The three on the recurrent branch of the median nerve — the 'OAF' of LOAF. Adductor pollicis is on the ulnar nerve, which is why thumb function splits between two nerves. Thenar wasting and weak palmar abduction = median at the wrist. Froment's sign (IP flexion to pinch) = ulnar loss of adductor pollicis.
Hypothenar eminence Abductor digiti minimi, flexor digiti minimi brevis, opponens digiti minimi (palmaris brevis in the skin). Okwumabua 2023 All on the deep (and palmaris brevis the superficial) branch of the ulnar nerve. Hypothenar wasting = ulnar at or above the wrist; a lesion in the deep branch alone spares hypothenar sensation.
Intrinsic-plus: lumbricals and interossei Lumbricals 1–4, dorsal interossei 1–4, palmar interossei 1–3. Launico 2025 Flex the MCP joints while extending the IP joints — the opposite of what the long flexors and extensor digitorum do at those joints. PAD and DAB about the middle finger. The claw hand: MCP hyperextended and IPs flexed. Worse in the ring and little fingers with an ulnar lesion at the wrist, because FDP to those fingers is intact and pulls harder (the ulnar paradox).
Hip, knee, ankle and foot
Hip flexors Iliopsoas (prime), rectus femoris, sartorius, tensor fasciae latae, pectineus, adductor longus and brevis. Iliopsoas is the only one that flexes the hip past 90° effectively; the others help from lower angles and add rotation or adduction. Weak hip flexion in sitting is iliopsoas; if the thigh rolls out it is sartorius substituting, rolled in it is TFL.
Hip abductors and pelvic stability Gluteus medius, gluteus minimus, TFL (and the upper fibres of gluteus maximus). In single-leg stance they hold the pelvis level against body weight trying to drop the opposite side — a lever problem, not a strength-to-move problem. Trendelenburg sign (pelvis drops on the unsupported side) or a compensated Trendelenburg gait (trunk leans over the weak hip to shorten the lever). Superior gluteal nerve.
Deep six lateral rotators Piriformis, superior gemellus, obturator internus, inferior gemellus, quadratus femoris, obturator externus. Chang 2023 Massel 2026 Larson 2023 Run horizontally behind the hip from pelvis to greater trochanter, top to bottom in that order. Rotate the hip laterally and hold the femoral head in the socket like a posterior cuff. Tested only as a group. The sciatic nerve usually passes below piriformis — the relationship behind piriformis syndrome, whose diagnostic tests are argued about. Contested
Gluteus maximus and hamstrings (hip extension) Gluteus maximus, semitendinosus, semimembranosus, biceps femoris long head, hamstring part of adductor magnus. Share hip extension. The hamstrings dominate in walking; gluteus maximus is recruited for climbing, rising and running. Gluteus maximus lurch — trunk thrown backward at heel strike to keep the hip extended — when gluteus maximus is weak. Hamstring cramping in a hip-extension test means the knee was left straight.
Quadriceps Rectus femoris, vastus lateralis, vastus medialis, vastus intermedius. Arain 2023 Biondi 2023 Four heads, one tendon to the patella and on to the tibial tuberosity; one nerve (femoral). Only rectus femoris crosses the hip. Knee buckling in stance and difficulty with stairs and sit-to-stand. Hamstrings Semitendinosus, semimembranosus, biceps femoris (long and short heads). Hu 2026 Vaughn 2023 Three muscles from the ischial tuberosity crossing hip and knee. Medial pair rotate the flexed knee in, biceps rotates it out. Short head of biceps is the odd one — one joint, fibular division. Strain at the proximal musculotendinous junction in sprinting; weakness on the fibular side only (short head) is a common fibular division lesion.
Pes anserinus (Say Grace before Tea) Sartorius, gracilis, semitendinosus. Attum 2022 Three tendons inserting together on the proximal medial tibia, from three compartments with three nerves (femoral, obturator, tibial). All flex the knee and rotate the flexed knee medially. Pain and tenderness just below the medial joint line — pes anserine bursitis and tendinopathy. They also resist valgus dynamically.
Hip adductors Adductor longus, brevis and magnus, pectineus, gracilis (obturator externus as a rotator). Ramage 2023 The medial compartment, mostly on the obturator nerve. Triceps surae Gastrocnemius (two heads) and soleus (and plantaris). Binstead 2026 Share the calcaneal tendon. Gastrocnemius crosses the knee and works best with it straight; soleus does not and works regardless. Stirrup of the foot Tibialis anterior (medial) and fibularis longus (lateral, crossing under the foot). Both insert on the medial cuneiform and the base of the 1st metatarsal, from opposite sides, forming a sling under the midfoot. One inverts, one everts; together they steady the arch. A flattened transverse arch and loss of 1st-ray stability; fibularis longus weakness shows as a first metatarsal that will not plantarflex.
Tarsal tunnel (Tom, Dick And Very Nervous Harry) Tibialis posterior, flexor digitorum longus, (posterior tibial artery, vein, tibial nerve), flexor hallucis longus. Pass behind the medial malleolus under the flexor retinaculum in that order, front to back. Burning in the sole with a positive Tinel's over the tunnel — tibial nerve compression; tibialis posterior tendon failure is the adult acquired flatfoot.
Anterior compartment — foot drop Tibialis anterior, extensor hallucis longus, extensor digitorum longus, fibularis tertius. Launico 2026 One compartment, one nerve (deep fibular), one job: lift the foot in swing and lower it after heel strike. Foot slap after heel strike and a steppage gait (high knee to clear the toes). With eversion also weak, the lesion is the common fibular nerve; with eversion spared, the deep fibular branch or L4–L5.
Lateral compartment — evertors Fibularis longus and brevis. Mahabadi 2023 Superficial fibular nerve; both pass behind the lateral malleolus. Weak eversion and recurrent inversion sprains; loss with an intact anterior compartment localizes to the superficial fibular branch.
Trunk, neck, jaw and breathing
Abdominal wall Rectus abdominis, external oblique, internal oblique, transversus abdominis. Scharbach 2023 Three flat layers with fibres at different angles — external oblique down and in (hands in pockets), internal oblique up and in, transversus horizontal — wrapped around a vertical rectus. All on T7–L1. Beevor's sign: the umbilicus moves upward on a curl-up when the lower abdominals are weak (a lesion around T10). Convention

Goldman 2023 Rathee 2024 All the muscles of mastication are on CN V3. Three close the mouth; the lateral pterygoid is the only one that opens it, pulling the condyle forward onto the eminence. Hip

Lateral rotation of the tibia (knee flexed) Biceps femoris. — Popliteus, medial hamstrings. L5–S2. Hip lateral rotation fakes it.
Ankle and foot
Dorsiflexion Tibialis anterior. EHL, EDL, fibularis tertius. Gastrocnemius, soleus, tibialis posterior, fibularis longus and brevis, FHL, FDL. L4–L5 (ISNCSCI L4 key muscle) Rupp 2021. Foot drop; steppage gait.

Trunk and neck

Neck extension Splenius capitis and cervicis, semispinalis, erector spinae (cervical). Upper trapezius, levator scapulae, suboccipitals. SCM, longus colli and capitis, scalenes. Posterior rami, CN XI. Thoracic extension substitutes.

Neck lateral flexion Scalenes, SCM. Splenius, upper trapezius, levator scapulae, rectus capitis lateralis. The same muscles of the other side. C3–C8, CN XI. Shoulder elevation brings the ear to the shoulder.

Every motor nerve — what it supplies and what its loss looks like

The nerve column of the muscle tables, turned inside out. The finding is the picture a lesion of that nerve produces — the pattern you recognise across the room before you test a single muscle — and the muscles listed are the ones that produce it. Root levels are the common textbook ranges; they vary by a segment between texts and between people.

NerveRootsMusclesWhat its loss looks like
Cervical and brachial plexus
Spinal accessory (CN XI) Cranial; C3–C4 proprioception. Sternocleidomastoid, trapezius. Shoulder droops, weak shrug, lateral winging, and weak head turning away from the lesion. The commonest cause is surgery in the posterior triangle.
Phrenic C3, C4, C5. Diaphragm. Paradoxical abdominal movement supine; one side is often asymptomatic, both sides cause breathlessness lying flat.
Dorsal scapular C4, C5. Rhomboids, levator scapulae. Mild medial border prominence and weak retraction; usually subtle.
Long thoracic C5, C6, C7. Serratus anterior. Medial winging — the medial border and inferior angle lift off the ribs on a wall push.
Suprascapular C5, C6. Supraspinatus, infraspinatus. Wasting above and below the spine of the scapula with weak abduction and lateral rotation; a lesion at the spinoglenoid notch wastes infraspinatus alone.
Nerve to subclavius C5, C6. Subclavius. No clinical picture.
Lateral pectoral C5, C6, C7. Pectoralis major (clavicular head, part of sternocostal). Flattening of the upper chest and weak adduction from flexion.
Medial pectoral C8, T1. Pectoralis minor, pectoralis major (sternocostal). Weak adduction from above; lower pectoral bulk lost.
Upper and lower subscapular C5, C6, C7. Subscapularis (both); teres major (lower). Weak medial rotation; rarely isolated.
Thoracodorsal C6, C7, C8. Latissimus dorsi. Weak extension–adduction and loss of the posterior axillary fold; cannot depress the shoulder to push up.
Axillary C5, C6. Deltoid, teres minor. Flattened shoulder contour and weak abduction beyond the first few degrees, with numbness over the lateral upper arm (regimental badge area). Shoulder dislocation and surgical neck fracture.
Musculocutaneous C5, C6, C7. Coracobrachialis, biceps, brachialis. Weak elbow flexion and supination, absent biceps reflex, numb lateral forearm; brachioradialis keeps some flexion.
Radial (above the elbow) C5–T1. Triceps, anconeus, brachioradialis, ECRL; then the posterior interosseous group. Wrist drop with finger drop and weak brachioradialis; triceps spared when the nerve is injured in the spiral groove (branches arise above it).
Posterior interosseous C7, C8. Supinator, ECRB (usually), ECU, extensor digitorum, EDM, extensor indicis, APL, EPB, EPL. Finger and thumb drop without wrist drop; the wrist extends with radial deviation because ECRL is spared and ECU is not. No sensory loss.
Median (above the elbow) C6–T1. Pronator teres, FCR, palmaris longus, FDS; then the anterior interosseous group and the thenar muscles. Hand of benediction on trying to make a fist: index and middle fingers stay straight. Pronation lost.
Anterior interosseous C7, C8, T1. FPL, FDP to index and middle, pronator quadratus. Cannot make an OK sign — the thumb IP and index DIP collapse into a pinch of the pads. No sensory loss.
Median (at the wrist) — recurrent branch C8, T1. APB, opponens pollicis, FPB (superficial head); lumbricals 1–2 from the digital branches. Thenar wasting, weak palmar abduction and opposition, numbness of the lateral three and a half digits — carpal tunnel syndrome.
Ulnar C7, C8, T1. FCU, FDP to ring and little; at the wrist the hypothenar muscles, palmaris brevis, interossei, lumbricals 3–4, adductor pollicis, FPB (deep head). Claw of the ring and little fingers, Froment's and Wartenberg's signs, first web wasting. A lesion at the elbow claws less than one at the wrist (ulnar paradox).
Lumbar and sacral plexus
Femoral L2, L3, L4. Iliacus, sartorius, pectineus, quadriceps, articularis genus. Knee buckling, weak knee extension and hip flexion, absent patellar reflex, numb anteromedial thigh and medial leg.
Obturator L2, L3, L4. Adductor longus, brevis, magnus (adductor part), gracilis, obturator externus, (pectineus). Weak adduction and a circumducted swing; numb patch on the medial thigh.
Superior gluteal L4, L5, S1. Gluteus medius, gluteus minimus, TFL. Trendelenburg sign and gait.
Inferior gluteal L5, S1, S2. Gluteus maximus. Difficulty rising from a chair and climbing stairs; backward trunk lurch in stance.
Nerves to the lateral rotators L4–S2. Piriformis (S1, S2), obturator internus and superior gemellus (L5–S2), quadratus femoris and inferior gemellus (L4–S1). No distinct clinical picture.
Sciatic — tibial division L4–S3. Semitendinosus, semimembranosus, biceps femoris long head, adductor magnus (hamstring part); continues as the tibial nerve. Weak knee flexion with the tibial nerve deficits below.
Sciatic — common fibular division L4–S2. Biceps femoris short head; continues as the common fibular nerve. Foot drop with weakness of the short head of biceps — the sign that the lesion is above the knee, not at the fibular head.
Tibial L4–S3. Gastrocnemius, soleus, plantaris, popliteus, tibialis posterior, FDL, FHL; then the plantar nerves. Cannot rise on the toes, weak inversion and toe flexion, absent ankle reflex, numb sole.
Common fibular L4–S2. Divides into the deep and superficial fibular nerves. Foot drop with weak eversion and a numb dorsum of the foot; inversion is spared (tibialis posterior) — which separates it from an L5 root lesion, where inversion is weak too.
Deep fibular L4, L5, S1. Tibialis anterior, EHL, EDL, fibularis tertius, EDB, EHB. Foot drop with eversion spared; numb first web space.
Superficial fibular L5, S1, S2. Fibularis longus, fibularis brevis. Weak eversion; numb dorsum of the foot except the first web.
Medial plantar S1–S3 (texts differ). Abductor hallucis, FDB, FHB, lumbrical 1. Burning in the medial sole; motor loss rarely noticed.
Lateral plantar S1–S3 (texts differ). Quadratus plantae, abductor digiti minimi, FDM brevis, adductor hallucis, interossei, lumbricals 2–4. Clawing of the lesser toes over time.
Pudendal S2, S3, S4. External anal sphincter, external urethral sphincter, perineal muscles, part of levator ani. Faecal and urinary incontinence and perineal numbness — with saddle anaesthesia, a cauda equina red flag.
Trunk and cranial
Posterior rami of the spinal nerves Every segment. Erector spinae, transversospinalis, interspinales, splenius, suboccipitals (C1). Segmental and hard to see — the back muscles overlap many levels.
Thoraco-abdominal, subcostal, first lumbar (iliohypogastric, ilioinguinal) T7–L1. Rectus abdominis, obliques, transversus abdominis, pyramidalis. Beevor's sign; a segmental bulge of the abdominal wall after a thoracic nerve injury.
Intercostal T1–T11. Intercostals, subcostales, transversus thoracis, serratus posterior superior. Paradoxical intercostal indrawing in high spinal cord injury.
Ansa cervicalis C1, C2, C3. Sternohyoid, omohyoid, sternothyroid (thyrohyoid and geniohyoid by C1 with CN XII). No distinct bedside picture.
Oculomotor (CN III) Midbrain. Superior, inferior and medial rectus, inferior oblique, levator palpebrae superioris. Eye down and out, ptosis, and a dilated pupil when compression reaches the parasympathetic fibres.
Trochlear (CN IV) Midbrain. Superior oblique. Vertical double vision looking down and in, head tilted away from the lesion.
Trigeminal, mandibular division (CN V3) Pons. Masseter, temporalis, pterygoids, mylohyoid, anterior digastric, tensor veli palatini, tensor tympani. Jaw deviates toward the weak side on opening; wasting of the temple and cheek.
Abducens (CN VI) Pons. Lateral rectus. Cannot abduct the eye; horizontal double vision. A false localizing sign of raised intracranial pressure.
Facial (CN VII) Pons. Muscles of facial expression, platysma, stapedius, stylohyoid, posterior digastric. Lower motor neuron: the whole side of the face, forehead included. Upper motor neuron: the lower face only. Hyperacusis if the lesion is proximal to the nerve to stapedius.
Glossopharyngeal (CN IX) Medulla. Stylopharyngeus. Reduced gag sensation on one side; motor loss is hard to see.
Vagus (CN X) Medulla. Palate (except tensor), pharyngeal constrictors, palatoglossus, larynx. Uvula deviates away from the weak side, nasal speech, hoarse voice, weak cough, dysphagia.
Hypoglossal (CN XII) Medulla. Genioglossus, hyoglossus, styloglossus, intrinsic tongue muscles. Tongue deviates toward the weak side, with wasting and fasciculation on that side in a lower motor neuron lesion.

What to practice

Built from the cells you marked missed while running recall. Nothing here is pre-filled - if this is empty, go and quiz yourself first. The practice method is chosen by what the item is, not by what kind of learner you are: a number, a pair of hands and a decision are three different jobs and they need three different kinds of rehearsal.

Why this is not a learning-styles quiz. Sorting students into visual, auditory or kinesthetic learners and matching teaching to the label is one of the most durable ideas in education that the evidence does not support - the studies that test it properly find no benefit from the match Pashler 2008. What does hold up is the method fitting the material: retrieving a fact from memory beats re-reading it, and spacing the attempts beats massing them Dunlosky 2013. So the plan below sorts your misses by the kind of thing they are.
QuestionAnswer
Which three muscles form the scapular upward rotation force couple, and what does each pull? Upper trapezius (acromion up and in), lower trapezius (root of the spine down and in), serratus anterior (inferior angle forward and out). Together they rotate the glenoid upward.
A patient's scapula lifts off the ribs at its medial border when they push against a wall. Which nerve? Long thoracic (C5–C7) — serratus anterior. Lateral winging with a drooped shoulder would be the spinal accessory nerve.
Name the rotator cuff muscles in the order of their insertion on the greater tubercle, and the odd one out. Supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor (superior, middle, inferior facets); subscapularis inserts on the lesser tubercle.
Which elbow flexor are you biasing with the forearm in mid-position, and which nerve supplies it? Brachioradialis — radial nerve. Biceps and brachialis are on the musculocutaneous nerve.
How do you stop FDP from contributing to an FDS test? Hold every other finger in full extension — FDP's tendons share a common belly and cannot flex one finger alone — and watch that the DIP stays floppy.
What is Froment's sign, and which muscle is it substituting for? Thumb IP flexion when pinching paper — flexor pollicis longus (median) substituting for a weak adductor pollicis (ulnar).
Which hand intrinsics are on the median nerve? LOAF: lumbricals 1–2, opponens pollicis, abductor pollicis brevis, flexor pollicis brevis (superficial head). Everything else is ulnar.
Why is gluteus maximus tested with the knee flexed? Knee flexion shortens the hamstrings so they are too slack to extend the hip — the test then loads gluteus maximus.
A patient's pelvis drops on the left when they stand on the right leg. Which muscle and nerve? Right gluteus medius (and minimus) — right superior gluteal nerve (L4–S1). The stance side's abductors hold the opposite side of the pelvis up.
Name the pes anserinus muscles and their three nerves. Sartorius (femoral), gracilis (obturator), semitendinosus (tibial).
Which hamstring is not supplied by the tibial division of the sciatic nerve? The short head of biceps femoris — common fibular division.
Foot drop with normal inversion: root or nerve? Fibular nerve. Tibialis posterior (tibial nerve, L4–L5) still inverts. An L5 root lesion weakens inversion too, because it reaches both nerves.
Why are the plantarflexors tested with single-leg heel raises rather than by hand? An examiner's arm cannot break even a weakened plantarflexor, so manual resistance grades nearly everyone 5. Body weight is a load the muscle can fail against.
What is Beevor's sign? The umbilicus moves upward during a curl-up because the lower abdominals are weak and the upper ones are not — a lesion around T10.
Which neck muscle turns the face to the opposite side, and what nerve supplies it? Sternocleidomastoid — spinal accessory nerve (CN XI). Splenius capitis turns it to the same side.
Which way does the jaw deviate with a right trigeminal motor lesion? To the right — the intact left lateral pterygoid pushes the left condyle forward unopposed.
A patient with facial weakness can wrinkle the forehead on both sides. Upper or lower motor neuron? Upper — the forehead has cortical input from both hemispheres, so a central lesion spares it. A Bell's palsy takes the forehead too.
Which extraocular muscle is tested by looking in and down, and which nerve supplies it? Superior oblique — trochlear nerve (CN IV).
On saying 'ah', the uvula deviates to the left. Which side is weak? The right — the intact left side pulls the uvula toward itself (CN X).
Name the ten ISNCSCI key muscle functions and their levels. C5 elbow flexors, C6 wrist extensors, C7 elbow extensors, C8 middle finger flexors, T1 little finger abductors, L2 hip flexors, L3 knee extensors, L4 ankle dorsiflexors, L5 long toe extensors, S1 ankle plantar flexors Rupp 2021.

About this guide. Version 1.0 · September 2026 The organisation, the "what fakes it" column, the relationship tables, the drill and both diagrams are original to this guide. The origins, insertions, innervations and actions are standard descriptive anatomy written for this guide, each row citing the free StatPearls chapter that covers that muscle so it can be checked; they have not yet been checked against those chapters line by line. Where standard texts disagree — root levels for supinator, the long toe flexors and the plantar intrinsics, the nerve to pectineus, the split supply of flexor pollicis brevis — the row is marked Contested and says how. The 0–5 grades and the ten key muscles are quoted from the international spinal cord injury standard (Rupp 2021). Manual muscle test positions, stabilization points and grading modifiers follow the widely taught conventions of the standard muscle-testing texts, are marked Convention, and are not quoted from any source.
