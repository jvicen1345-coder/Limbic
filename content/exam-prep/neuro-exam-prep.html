1AWhat neurologic PT covers, and where its practice resources come from
Break it down

The gistNeurologic PT is the evaluation and treatment of people with movement problems caused by disease or injury of the nervous system. The Academy of Neurologic Physical Therapy (ANPT) publishes the practice resources.

Watch forEDGE = Evaluation Database to Guide Effectiveness. EDGE documents exist for PD, MS, TBI, SCI, stroke and vestibular disorders.

  • Definition: "evaluation and treatment of individuals with movement problems due to disease or injury of the nervous system." 1
  • ANPT (neuropt.org): many resources free to members and nonmembers, including student blogs and podcasts, with more access for members; publishes the Journal of Neurologic Physical Therapy, clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) and EDGE documents.
  • CPGs: vestibular hypofunction (Apr 2016, revised Apr 2022), core outcome measures (Jul 2018), concussion (Apr 2020), locomotion in chronic stroke/SCI/TBI (Jan 2020), AFOs and FES post-stroke (Apr 2021), Parkinson disease (Jan 2022); balance and falls, and locomotion in subacute stroke/SCI/TBI, in process.
  • Person-first language inference (answers to the fill-in exercise): "stroke patient" → person with (or after) a stroke; "dizzy patient" → person with dizziness; "spastic arm" → arm with spasticity; "Parki's" → person with Parkinson disease; "he's a TBI" → he has a TBI. Person-first language is the standard in government documents, scientific journals and UN publications. Some communities, such as the Deaf community, prefer not to use it. 2

1CClinical reasoning vs clinical decision-making
Break it down

The gistClinical reasoning is the thinking process. Decision-making is the choice it leads to: treat, or refer, co-manage or consult.

Watch forClinical reasoning in this framing emphasizes movement analysis and is an interactive, patient-collaboration process.

  • Clinical reasoning: a complex process used to identify problem-framing, problem-solving and decision-making, the active pathology, the reason for the problem, and the consequences of the illness or disease process. It emphasizes movement analysis and is interactive, in collaboration with the patient. 3
  • Decision-making: the PT decides whether to provide interventions or refer to one or more providers for management, co-management or consultation. 1
  • Models: HOAC-II (Hypothesis-Oriented Algorithm for Clinicians II) 4; Patient/Client Management model from the APTA Guide 5; Integrated Framework 6; ICF-based tool for reasoning and reflection 7; revised Integrated Framework 8.
1DThe streamlined clinical reasoning and decision-making cycle
Break it down

The gistA loop with shared decision-making at its center: interview → exam → evaluation → plan of care → reassessment → outcome, with reflection between every step.

Watch forHypotheses (Ho) are formed early (initial observation, interview) and guide which tests you pick. They aren't something you form only at the end.

  • Initial impression → Interview/history: focused questions, review of systems, formulate Ho.
  • Exam: systems review, task and movement analysis, outcome measures, impairment testing.
  • Evaluation: diagnosis (plan and referral), prognosis and goals, problems (PIP, N-PIP, AP), plan of care.
  • POC/interventions → Reassessment (outcome measures) → Outcome (goals met or modified).
  • Reflection sits between each step; patient and therapist share decisions at the center. 4, 6, 7, 8
  • Hypothesis: a diagnostic idea that may identify pathology, impairments, functional deficits, and causes of and factors influencing disability and ability. It guides data collection and treatment; consider the ICF.
  • Ho process (also a way to structure the evaluation): initial observation → Ho → subjective exam → Ho → task/movement analysis → Ho of potential impairments → test of potential impairments, with reflection throughout. 1
1EPIP vs N-PIP vs AP
Break it down

The gistProblems either exist now or may happen later. Existing problems need remediation; future ones need prevention.

Watch forA family member or the care team can identify a problem the patient doesn't report: that is an N-PIP, not a PIP.

  • Patient-identified problems (PIP): reported by the patient, usually functional limitations and disabilities; may exist now (react) or be anticipated. The therapist generates hypotheses about their cause and sets testing criteria to judge outcomes and whether the hypothesis was right.
  • Non-patient-identified problems (N-PIP): functional problems not reported by the patient, identified by the therapist, other team members, or family/care providers; may exist now or be anticipated.
  • Anticipated problems (AP): may occur in the future; require prevention and education, so you are proactive instead of reactive. 4
  • 2 ways to manage: existing problems → remediation; future problems → prevention. 1

1GReflection, the expert clinician, and the four ways a PT can involve others
Break it down

The gistReflection happens in action (during) or on action (after). Experts self-monitor and reflect well. When care goes beyond you, you co-manage, consult, manage or refer.

Watch forManage means you stay accountable while using assistive personnel. Refer means the need is outside your personal, jurisdictional or professional scope, or you want specific testing.

  • Reflection: develops clinical reasoning and professional growth. In action = during the event (e.g., thinking out loud). On action = after the event, internally or externally focused (e.g., a journal entry). 7, 9
  • Expert clinician: patient empowerment; collaborative problem-solving; caring, humble, loves clinical care; recognizes many presentations; good self-monitoring and reflection; prioritizes and modifies interventions efficiently; fast, efficient work and problem-solving; commitment to professional growth; deep understanding of the complexities of care; understands the patient's viewpoint and the wider implications; inquisitive learner with area-specific knowledge; limited delegation of care. 10, 11
  • Co-manage: collaborate with other professionals to direct or coordinate management. Consult: give or receive expert opinion. Manage: remain accountable when using assistive personnel. Refer: send to another provider for services outside your scope, or for specific testing. 5
1HOutcome measures: purpose, selection and MDC vs MCID
Break it down

The gistOutcome measures discriminate, predict or evaluate. Choose them deductively, narrowing the list at each step of the visit.

Watch forMDC = smallest change that is a true change beyond error. MCID = smallest change that is meaningful to the person.

  • Purposes: discriminate (aid clinical decisions, POC, diagnosis); predict (goal setting, communicating with patients, providers and payers, predicting future status, motivating); evaluate (track change, determine treatment effectiveness).
  • Deductive selection: referral → initial list → refine after initial observation → refine after history → refine after systems review → final selection.
  • Factors: what to measure (ICF: body function/structure, activity, participation); purpose; type (disease-specific vs generic; performance-based vs self-report); patient and clinic factors (patient goals and perceptions, clinic requirements); psychometrics (reliability, validity for the population, diagnostic accuracy = sensitivity and specificity, responsiveness = MDC and MCID); feasibility (time, space, equipment, training, cost or proprietary, culture/language). 12
  • MDC (minimal detectable change): the minimum change needed to exceed measurement error and variability, i.e., the smallest change that is a true change. MCID (minimal clinically important difference): the smallest difference considered worthwhile or important, which tells you whether a change is meaningful. 1
  • Considerations: performance-based measures may not reflect actual performance at home and in the community; consider the population a measure was developed on; no measure does it all, so combine them (e.g., activity + participation + body structure; performance + self-report; steady-state + anticipatory + reactive balance). 1

2AHow to run the subjective exam
Break it down

The gistRespect plus three P's: introduce yourself, ask how the person wants to be addressed, protect privacy, position both of you well, and pace it.

Watch forEmpower the patient first, then ask permission to involve family or caregivers.

  • Respect: self-introduction; ask how the patient wishes to be addressed; family/caregivers.
  • Privacy.
  • Position: patient in a supported position (back, feet, arms), head neutral; PT in the patient's line of vision, at the same level.
  • Pace: allow time for the patient to respond.
  • Focus: equipment, life roles, social support and environment (ICF categories), and social determinants of health. 1
  • ICF model: health condition (disorder or disease) at the top; body functions and structures ↔ activities ↔ participation in the middle; environmental and personal factors underneath, feeding all three.
  • SDOH, 5 domains (Healthy People 2030): economic stability; education access and quality; health care access and quality; neighborhood and built environment; social and community context.
2BSubjective pearls: PLOF, PSFS and shared decision-making
Break it down

The gistFor chronic conditions, anchor PLOF in time. Use standardized tools for goals (PSFS) and self-reported outcomes.

Watch forPSFS numbers differ by population: stroke MDC 1.94 / MCID 1.58; MS MDC 2.1 / MCID 2.5.

  • PLOF in a chronic neuro condition: ask about 6 months ago, 1 year ago, or before the most recent change.
  • Standardized subjective tools: patient goals via the Patient-Specific Functional Scale (PSFS); self-reported outcomes such as participation measures (PSFS, Stroke Impact Scale) and self-rating scales (e.g., fatigue).
  • To optimize shared decision-making, seek the patient's preferences. 1
  • PSFS form: the patient names up to 3 activities (up to 5 on extended versions) they have trouble with; each is rated 0 = unable to perform to 10 = able to perform at the same level as before the injury or problem; total score = sum of activity scores ÷ number of activities. Complete it at the end of the history, before the physical exam. Original MDC (90% CI): 2 points for the average score, 3 points for a single activity (Stratford 1995, as cited). 1

2CQuestion sets that bring out the patient's perspective
Break it down

The gistSix question groups: why they came, goals, roles, resources and constraints, barriers and facilitators, and readiness and learning style.

Watch forIf a patient can't state goals, probe: "What do you want to do better, or faster?" "Is there anything you don't do but would like to?" "What gives you joy?"

  • Why seeking care: what would you like to do; when could you last do it; how limiting is it; how long; what do you think contributes; other health conditions.
  • Goal setting: what do you hope to achieve; what benchmarks would show progress.
  • Role in society: what roles do you play at home and work; how do the problems interfere with them.
  • Resources and constraints: prior PT experience, knowledge of the condition, recent activity; daily help from family and friends; additional help needed; access to care (finances, distance, transportation, schedule, insurance).
  • Barriers and solutions: what might make it difficult to participate; what facilitates reaching goals.
  • Responsibility and learning: readiness to take an active role; comfort changing a behavior; learning preference (verbal, written, by doing). 8

2ETask analysis vs movement analysis
Break it down

The gistTask analysis asks how the person did the task (steps, devices, effectiveness, efficiency, safety). Movement analysis looks at the quality of the movement while you watch.

Watch forMovement analysis starts at the first observation (when the person enters, after the subjective and baseline vitals) and continues through exam and treatment.

  • Task analysis: what steps and in what order; what items/devices; how effective, efficient and safe. Tasks are affected by cognitive demands, familiarity/experience and environment. Example: getting out of bed by side-lying to sit, supine to sit, or a "flat spin"; making vegetable soup from a can, from frozen vegetables, or from fresh-cut vegetables.
  • Context: the 2013 APTA vision, "transforming society by optimizing movement to improve the human experience", casts PTs as movement system experts. Movement analysis = observing movement during tasks and activities; movement system diagnoses categorize problems by impairments and limitations of movement. 1
  • Movement analysis occurs during observation of the task. Influencing factors: starting posture, reflexes, environment (space, time constraints, external forces), emotional state, sensory input, repetition/experience, neural pathways, cardiopulmonary and MSK systems.
  • Observable constructs: symmetry, speed, amplitude/trajectory, alignment, verticality, stability, smoothness, sequencing, timing, accuracy, force production, force sustainability, symptom provocation.
  • When: early; the PT generates hypotheses during observation, which guides test selection. 1, 15

Break it down

2HCompensations, task regression and the ANPT core tasks
Break it down

The gistIf they succeed with a compensation, ask them to repeat it without. If they fail, regress in a fixed order: verbal cue → tactile cue → physical assistance.

Watch forIf a person can't modify the pattern with cueing or instruction, they are likely unable to fractionate and are synergistically bound.

  • Compensation example: in sit to stand, the more impacted foot is placed forward. Ask them to bring it back in line and try again.
  • Regression order: 1) add a verbal cue; 2) add a tactile cue; 3) add physical assistance, noting how much, how and where. 15
  • 6 ANPT core tasks for movement analysis: sitting, sit to stand, standing, walking (including a turn), step up/down, reach/grasp/manipulate. 15, 16
  • Functional status measurement: time, distance, assist level, assistive devices, environment, quality (movement analysis); tasks include rolling, supine to sit, transfers (bed, toilet, shower, floor) and wheelchair mobility; gait in m/s with deviations. 1

3BVisual perception: closure, figure-ground, neglect and depth
Break it down

The gistThree perceptual impairments each have a functional signature. Neglect is screened with line bisection and star cancellation.

Watch forNeglect means the person doesn't attend to part of the environment: trips, leaves food, misses meds. That is different from a field cut the person knows about. inference

  • Visual perceptual screen covers visual closure, figure-ground, visual neglect, apraxia and agnosia. 1
  • Visual closure: difficulty recognizing partially obscured objects.
  • Figure-ground: difficulty locating objects on a busy background (e.g., the peeler in a cluttered drawer).
  • Visual neglect: does not attend to a portion of the environment; may trip, not finish food, miss meds. Screening: line bisection and star cancellation. 19
  • Depth perception (stereopsis): the brain perceives depth from both eyes working together to form a 3-D image. Relevant to falls, stairs, tripping, driving and wheelchair maneuvering. 1
3CApraxia: ideational vs ideomotor, and how to test it
Break it down

The gistApraxia is a disorder of skilled purposeful movement from impaired motor planning, not from comprehension or sensorimotor loss.

Watch forIdeational = can't sequence a multi-step activity or know how to use a tool. Ideomotor = can't do it on command but may do it spontaneously later.

  • Ideational: inability to coordinate activities with multiple sequential movements (dressing, eating, bathing); e.g., seals the envelope before writing the letter; doesn't know how to use a tool.
  • Ideomotor: inability to produce expected movements to a verbal command; e.g., can't use scissors on command but later picks them up and uses them spontaneously. 20
  • Clinical test: ask the patient to wave goodbye, beckon "come here", salute, hitch a lift; show how they would use a comb, toothbrush, scissors, or hammer a nail. If they fail, mimic it for them. 1
3DMoCA: what it screens, the cut-off, and how to document it
Break it down

The gistA screen for mild cognitive impairment (MCI) across 7 domains. It does not diagnose.

Watch forDocument "positive screen for MCI", never "has MCI". Add 1 point if < 12 years of education. The blind version is scored out of 22.

  • Domains: short-term memory; visuospatial (clock drawing, cube copy); executive function (trail making, phonemic fluency, verbal abstraction); attention; concentration and working memory; language (confrontation naming of animals, sentence repetition); orientation.
  • Cut-off: < 26 = positive screen for MCI; sensitivity 51%, specificity 96%, LR 13.5. Clinically meaningful difference: 1.7 points. 21
  • Documentation: < 26 → "positive screen for mild cognitive impairment" (can't say the patient has MCI). > 26 → "negative screen" (can't say cognition is normal).
  • Formal certification training takes about an hour (mocacognition.com). 1 Source conflict The wording gives < 26 as positive and > 26 as negative, which leaves a score of exactly 26 unstated; by the < 26 cut-off, 26 is a negative screen. inference
3ECranial nerves I and II: smell, acuity, pupils and fields
Break it down

The gistCN I: one nostril at a time, eyes closed, non-noxious smells. CN II: Snellen acuity, swinging flashlight, confrontation fields.

Watch forIn the swinging flashlight test, CN II is judged by the contralateral pupil (consensual) and CN III by the ipsilateral pupil.

  • Why screen CNs: referral, baseline (neurodegenerative disease), assessing recovery (Bell's palsy), guiding treatment (vestibular, vision), screening for pathology (PD, AD, COVID-19). 1
  • CN I (olfactory): eyes closed, close one nostril, distinctive non-noxious smell (coffee, vanilla, pepper, mint, soap); identify and compare sides. Document e.g. "+ vanilla L = R; + coffee L, − coffee R".
  • CN II acuity: Snellen chart, one eye at a time, glasses on if worn; lowest line with most optotypes read; e.g., "L 20/30; R 20/30", noting corrective lenses.
  • Pupils (II & III): shine the penlight obliquely, swinging eye to eye every 2–3 s; the contralateral pupil should constrict. Present, absent or diminished.
  • Visual fields (confrontation): patient looks at your nose and covers one eye; you cover your opposite eye; wiggle fingers at arm's length (~2 ft), bring in slowly; test superior/inferior nasal and temporal quadrants. Normal: superior 60°, inferior 75°, nasal 60°, temporal 100°. Document full or restricted. 1
3FCranial nerves III, IV and VI: lids, the "H", convergence and saccades
Break it down

The gistIII opens the lid and moves the eye in most directions; IV handles down-and-in; VI handles lateral gaze. Test them together.

Watch forConvergence is intact if focus holds from 24" to 4". Saccade targets are held no more than 40° apart; normal = on target in 1–2 reps.

  • Eyelid (III): observe for ptosis (palpebral fissure asymmetry); ask for upward gaze without moving the head.
  • "H" pattern: head still, move slowly; note ROM, weakness direction or saccades (e.g., "impaired R downward gaze").
  • Convergence/divergence: start 2 ft away, bring the target toward the nose until blurry/double; measure the distance. Intact to 4"; impaired if blurry or double > 4". Then follow the target out for divergence. Also note saccades, or pupils that don't constrict as the target comes in.
  • Volitional saccades: finger and pen ≤ 40° apart; "look at my finger… pen… finger"; check horizontal, vertical, diagonal. Impaired if overshoot/undershoot, slow, delayed, jerky or ratchet-like. Not indicated with eye muscle paralysis; saccade problems can be central, so differential diagnosis is needed.
  • IV (trochlear) = diagonal downward-medial movement; VI (abducens) = lateral deviation; both assessed during the H, convergence and saccades. 1
3GCranial nerves V and VII: face sensation, chewing and facial expression
Break it down

The gistV = sensation to the face (3 divisions) plus masseter and lateral pterygoid. VII = facial movement plus taste on the anterior 2/3 of the tongue.

Watch forTest V sensation on the forehead, cheek and jaw. Test VII with smile, eyebrows, pursed lips, tight eye closure and platysma.

  • V sensory: eyes closed; cotton wisp on a known intact area first, then forehead, cheek, jaw; sharp/dull; tactile extinction. Intact, diminished/impaired or absent.
  • V motor: palpate masseters for atrophy and during clench/unclench; lateral pterygoids: push the jaw forward with the mouth slightly open, watch for deviation. Normal, weak or absent.
  • VII motor: smile widely (zygomaticus); wrinkle forehead (frontalis); purse lips (orbicularis oris); close eyes tightly while you try to open them (orbicularis oculi); "draw down hard on the corners of your mouth" (platysma). Compare upper and lower face.
  • Document VII motor as normal, weak or absent.
  • VII taste (often not tested): sweet, sour and salty on the anterior 2/3; test sweet on the tip of the tongue, eyes closed; intact or absent. 1
3HCranial nerve VIII: hearing, gaze holding, DVA and the head impulse test
Break it down

The gistHearing: finger rub, Weber, Rinne. Vestibular: gaze holding, dynamic visual acuity, head impulse test.

Watch forHIT: a corrective saccade back to target means likely peripheral vestibular loss on the side the head was turned toward. Head turned R, eyes correct L → "+R HIT".

  • Finger rub: compare L vs R; intact, impaired or absent.
  • Weber: 256 or 512 Hz fork on the vertex. Negative (normal) = heard midline. Positive = lateralizes: ipsilateral conductive loss or contralateral sensorineural loss. Document the side it lateralizes to.
  • Rinne (tests conductive loss): fork on the mastoid, then 2.5 cm from the ear. Negative (normal) = air conduction louder than bone (AC > BC). Positive = bone louder or longer than air = conductive loss on the tested side.
  • Gaze holding: target 12–15" away, hold 5–15 s. Negative: moves both eyes through range, no diplopia, no nystagmus at 25–30°, < 3 beats at end range. Positive: > 3 beats at end range, any nystagmus at 25–30°, diplopia, or unable to reach ranges (lack of upward gaze is a central sign). End-gaze nystagmus may occur beyond 30°. Document − or + with the eye position.
  • DVA: static acuity first (may miss 1 optotype per line); flex head 30°, oscillate at 2 Hz (10–20° each way) while reading; ≥ 3-line drop = VOR impairment. Yaw or pitch.
  • HIT: head flexed 30°, eyes on your nose; after slow movements, a quick, small (5–10°) unexpected rotation. Negative = eyes stay on target. 1
3ICranial nerves IX–XII: palate, swallow, shrug and tongue
Break it down

The gistIX/X: voice, palate and uvula, swallowing, gag. XI: traps and SCM. XII: tongue protrusion and strength.

Watch forTongue deviates toward a CN XII (LMN) lesion but away from a cortical or corticobulbar lesion.

4EUpright Motor Control Test: purpose, setup and grading
Break it down

The gistThe UMCT tests voluntary control of the hemiparetic LE in upright, simulating gait. Flexion control (parts 1–3) = swing limb. Extension control (parts 4–6) = single-limb stance.

Watch forGrades are Weak / Moderate / Strong (plus Excessive and Unable to Test for extension). Up to 2 practice trials, then one graded trial per segment. Ankle dorsiflexion has no Moderate grade.

  • Point of the test: to identify patterned movement. Cue "soft knees" (don't lock the knees out) during standing. 1
  • Indications: CNS lesions with patterned movement or spasticity. Identifies the muscle groups most impaired in producing sufficient force or speed during stance or swing. The knee subtest can predict walking ability. 1, 26
  • Setup: examiner plus an assistant; patient must understand instructions and need no more than one person's assistance for single- or double-limb stance. 27
  • Flexion (3 reps, "as high and as fast as you can"): hip and knee: W = < 30° or > 10 s for 3 reps; M = 30–60° ×3 in 10 s; S = > 60° ×3 in 10 s. Ankle DF: W = < 90° or > 10 s; S = DF to a right angle or more ×3 in 10 s.
  • Hip extension: stand on the test limb; examiner reduces hand support. W = uncontrolled trunk flexion; M = can't hold fully erect but stops forward momentum, wobbles, or hyperextends trunk; S = trunk erect.
  • Knee extension: both knees at 30°, lift the stronger limb. W = continues to collapse; M = holds flexed knee without further collapse or heel rise; S = holds, then straightens on request; E = can't flex due to extensor thrust/tone; UT = no plantigrade foot. Knee flexion contracture caps the grade at M.
  • Ankle PF: knee at 0°, then heel rise. W = knee collapses/wobbles or extensor thrust; M = knee 0° and ankle 90° with vertical tibia; S = heel rise with knee at 0°; E = severe equinus or varus prevents a stable plantigrade ankle; UT = knee flexion contracture.
  • Reliability: 96% agreement (flexion), 90% (extension); gait prediction validity not established in this source. 27

Break it down

5CAttention types and memory systems
Break it down

The gistAttention runs from sustained (basic vigilance) to divided (multitasking). Memory splits into short-term, working and long-term, and long-term into declarative and non-declarative.

Watch forShort-term memory holds 7 ± 2 items. Working memory also manipulates them (prefrontal cortex). Procedural memory is non-declarative.

Attention = focusing on an object, person or activity at the expense of other information. It is biased, fragile, capacity-limited, changes across life, is needed for learning, and is pulled by internal and external distractions. 1

5DExecutive function and the three core EF skills
Break it down

The gistEF is a set of high-level supervisory abilities that generate, plan and monitor goal-directed behavior. Picture cognition as a building with attention as the foundation.

Watch forThe three core EF skills: inhibitory control, working memory and cognitive flexibility.

  • Definition: a set of high-level, interrelated supervisory abilities for generating, selecting, planning and monitoring goal-directed, adaptive responses; it initiates and sustains independent, purposeful or novel behavior; metacognition is thought to be essential to it. 5FApraxia of speech vs dysarthria
Break it down

The gistApraxia of speech is a planning (motor programming) problem. Dysarthria is an execution problem from weakness, abnormal tone or incoordination of speech muscles.

Watch forApraxia errors are inconsistent (better when automatic; groping; high awareness). Dysarthria errors are predictable and consistent across contexts.

  • Apraxia of speech: difficulty volitionally positioning, planning and sequencing speech movements; not due to weakness, slowness or discoordination; high awareness and frustration; inconsistent (may be fine when automatic); trial and error; groping; repetition helps; rarely isolated; minimal to profound; hard to separate from non-fluent aphasia, dysarthria and acquired stuttering.
  • Dysarthria: "slurred speech" from weakness, abnormal tone, incoordination or imprecise movement of the speech muscles; affects respiration, phonation, articulation, prosody, resonance. 5GAphasia: what it is, what it isn't, and fluent vs non-fluent
Break it down

5HAphasic error types and how to communicate with a person with aphasia
Break it down

The gistName the error: anomia, jargon, neologism, phonemic vs semantic paraphasia, telegraphic speech. Then adapt how you talk.

Watch forPhonemic paraphasia swaps sounds ("wishdasher"). Semantic paraphasia swaps words ("table" for "bed"). People with aphasia can look like they understand everything, because intonation and context are processed by the intact right hemisphere.

  • Comprehension: needs extra time; trouble with long, rapid or complex grammar (passive sentences), without visual support (phone), nonliteral language, competing conversation; lacks error awareness.
  • Expression: anomia (difficulty retrieving words); jargon/word salad (fluent but meaningless, real words lacking semantic meaning); neologisms (novel, unrecognizable words); phonemic and semantic paraphasias; telegraphic speech (omitting function words like "the", "of", "was"); grammatical errors; halting, effortful speech; single words or short, fragmented phrases.
  • Agraphia (written expression): trouble writing, typing or copying letters, words and sentences; single words only; letter or word substitutions; nonmeaningful syllables; incorrect grammar; letter formation. Alexia (reading comprehension): trouble recognizing words by sight, understanding written material from letters to books, sounding out words, function words (articles, prepositions, pronouns); misreading meaning ("couch" read as chair); slow and effortful.
  • Communication tips: quiet, fewer distractions, breaks for fatigue or frustration; relax and don't show concern about errors; allow time; speak as to a competent adult, not as to a child or someone deaf or cognitively impaired, and don't speak for them; verify ("I heard you say ___. Is that right?"); don't pretend to understand.
  • If they didn't understand: repeat, simplify, or say it another way; ask the same question several ways (yes/no may be inconsistent); short, concise speech; don't bombard; simple direct words without figures of speech; emphasize key words.
  • Encourage all modes: speaking, writing, drawing, yes/no, gestures, facial expressions. If stuck on a word: deep breath and retry; ask what it looks like, where it's found, what it's used for; ask if it's OK to come back to it; be kind and patient.
  • AAC uses residual language abilities and trains partners in augmented input to aid comprehension; offer written or visual choices so the person can show preferences, ideas and feelings (gestures, writing, pointing, books, devices); it isn't one-way and isn't the last step. 1, 30
  • SLP goals with a person with aphasia: maximize function; improve quality of life; assess emotional adjustment within scope; make family and friends better communication partners; help each person be the best communicator they can be with aphasia. 5IDysphagia: stages, stats, warning signs and silent aspiration
Break it down

The gistSwallowing has 4 stages. Stroke is the most common cause of neurogenic dysphagia, and about half of people with dysphagia can aspirate silently.

Watch forWarning signs include a wet voice after swallowing, fevers after eating, and recurrent pulmonary infections. MBSS and FEES are the instrumental tests.

  • Why it matters: early diagnosis and management improve quality of life and may prevent or delay death.
  • Stages: oral preparatory, oral transit/propulsion, pharyngeal, esophageal. ~50 pairs of striated cranial muscles are excited and inhibited in sequence from mouth to stomach; dysphagia is an impairment of this sensorimotor system.
  • Prevalence: 400,000–800,000 neurogenic cases per year worldwide; acute stroke ~65% (most common cause); PD ~50% (other studies up to ~90%); MS ~31.3%; dementia 13–57%; motor neuron disease 30–100% depending on type, all in later stages. 33
  • Instrumental tests: MBSS = modified barium swallow study; FEES = fiberoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing. Silent aspiration in ~50% of people with dysphagia: without a cough to warn you, aspiration goes unnoticed, so clinical signs and instrumental testing matter. inference 1
  • Risks: aspiration, lung inflammation and infection, respiratory distress, dehydration and malnutrition, lung scarring, fear/avoidance, chest pain.
  • Warning signs: coughing or throat clearing, wet voice, fevers after intake, congestion or wheezing after intake, drooling, unintentional weight loss, long meals, recurrent pneumonia, regurgitation, food sticking, fear of eating or pills.
  • Neurogenic changes: sensation (taste, smell, numbness, pocketing), oral weakness (poor lip seal → drooling, spillage), poor coordination (timing breath with swallow, moving food), trunk and neck weakness (positioning), cognitive issues (insight, memory, attention), impulsivity (gulping, rapid eating, large bites, bites while food is still in the mouth).
  • Aspiration = food or liquid misdirected into the lungs. Food "sticking" can come from pharyngeal weakness or discoordination, or from the esophagus (GI).
  • Aspiration pneumonia risk factors: dependence for feeding or oral care, poor oral hygiene, limited ambulation, compromised respiration or cough, prior aspiration pneumonia, compromised immunity, GI disease, current smoking. 1

6AGeneral exercise principles for neurologic populations
Break it down

The gistAerobic exercise primes the brain for motor learning. Strength training is effective and supported. Make it task-specific, and dose with FITT.

Watch forAerobic exercise has direct effects (neurotrophic factors such as BDNF, neurotransmitters, neuroplasticity) and indirect effects (fitness, blood flow, less inflammation), both feeding brain health.

Direct: ↑ neurotrophic factors (BDNF, NT-3) → ↑ neuroplasticity, neurogenesis and neuroprotection; ↑ neurotransmitters (dopamine, serotonin). Result: ↑ cognitive function (learning, memory, attention), ↑ mood, ↑ arousal, ↓ neurodegeneration. 34
  • Strength/resistance: evidence supports it in neurological disorders; maximizes functional capacity; prevents or reverses inactivity effects and comorbidities.
  • Task-specific and functional; FITT = frequency, intensity, time, type. 1

9AS and O in a neuro treatment note
Break it down

The gistS is what the patient says at the start. O is the session in chronological order, showing skilled PT and progress.

Break it down

The gistGroup O entries by therapeutic exercise, neuromuscular re-education, functional/therapeutic activities, and education/HEP, and state the purpose of each.

9CA and P
Break it down

The gistA is your professional judgment of progress, not a summary of O. P is what you will do next visit.

Watch forUseful A language: increase safety in home or community, reduce fall risk, reduce caregiver burden, reduced reliance on compensations.

  • A: functional improvement this session ("good progress with…; progress limited due to…"); factors helping or interfering with goals; not a restatement of O.

Break it down

The gistAcute care evaluations lean on discharge planning: home setup, support, vitals across activity, lines and tubes, and functional mobility.

9FWriting patient-centered functional goals
Break it down

The gistA goal names a meaningful activity the patient can't do now but wants to, written so anyone can observe and measure it: who, does what, under what conditions, how well, by when.

Watch forGoals are about function, never about an impairment for its own sake: never "decrease spasticity". Write what the patient will do, not what they will "be able to" do.

  • Functional goal: an individually meaningful activity the person can't perform because of their condition but wants to accomplish through PT; observable, repeatable, with a definite beginning and end; self-care, work or leisure. 70
  • Five parts: (1) Who: the patient; (2) What: the observable activity (the third word of the goal names it; avoid "will be able to"); (3) Conditions: environment, device, surface, setup or precautions specific to this patient; (4) How well: describe the help or accuracy concretely (where you assist, how often you cue, how many successful trials), not just "min A"; (5) By when: a target date from healing, evidence, experience and the patient's progress. 70
  • SMART check: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Realistic/Relevant, Timed. Set goals with the patient. 71
  • Where they come from: the patient's own goals (PSFS, goal-setting questions) for realistic, patient-centered goals 1; SDOH and environment shape prognosis and goals 1; acute care plans list short- and long-term goals 1; with hypertonia, goals stay functional and never target spasticity itself 1.
  • Making "how well" measurable: tie it to a test with a known meaningful change (e.g., a PSFS gain of at least the MCID: 1.58 after stroke, 2.5 in MS) or to time, distance, repetitions, assist level and cue frequency. inference 13, 14
  • Short- vs long-term: the long-term goal is the function you expect at discharge; short-term goals are the steps toward it, often the same task with more help, an easier condition or a lower standard. inference
  • example Strong: "Mr. R will transfer from bed to wheelchair toward his L (more involved) side by stand pivot, with contact guard at the trunk and one verbal cue for hand placement, in 4 of 5 trials within 3 weeks." Weak: "Pt will decrease tone in L elbow flexors" (impairment, not function) or "Pt will be able to walk better" (not observable or measurable).

  1. Cueing tells you about fractionationIn movement analysis, regress with verbal → tactile → physical cues (Topic 2). If the person still can't change the pattern, they're likely synergistically bound (Topic 4), which means MMT isn't appropriate and the UMCT or Fugl-Meyer is the better tool.

PIP: reported by the patient; current or anticipated; the therapist hypothesizes causes and sets testing criteria. N-PIP: identified by the therapist, team or family, not the patient. 03Name the factors to consider when selecting an outcome measure, and define MDC and MCID.
What to measure (ICF level); purpose (discriminative, predictive, evaluative); type (disease-specific vs generic, performance vs self-report); patient and clinic factors; psychometrics (reliability, validity, diagnostic accuracy, responsiveness); feasibility (time, space, equipment, training, cost, language). MDC = smallest change beyond error; MCID = smallest meaningful change. 12
04Contrast task analysis and movement analysis, and list the movement continuum phases.
Task analysis: how the person did the task: steps and order, devices, effectiveness, efficiency, safety. Movement analysis: quality during observation, using observable constructs (symmetry, speed, alignment, stability, timing…). Phases: initial conditions, preparation, initiation, execution, termination. 1, 15
05How do you regress a task during analysis, and what does failure to respond to cueing tell you?
Regress in order: verbal cue → tactile cue → physical assistance, noting how much, how and where assistance is applied. If the person can't modify the movement pattern with cueing or instruction, they are likely unable to fractionate and are synergistically bound. 1, 15
06How do you administer and document the MoCA?
Screens for MCI across 7 domains (short-term memory, visuospatial, executive, attention/working memory, language, orientation); does not diagnose. < 26 = positive screen (sens 51%, spec 96%, LR 13.5). Add 1 point if < 12 years of education; MoCA Blind is out of 22. Document “positive (or negative) screen for mild cognitive impairment”, never “has MCI”. 1, 21
07Describe the head impulse test and dynamic visual acuity test and how to interpret each.
HIT: sit lower than the patient; head flexed 30°; eyes on your nose; after slow movements, a quick, small (5–10°) unexpected rotation. A corrective saccade back to target = likely peripheral vestibular loss on the side of the head turn (head R, eyes correct L → +R HIT). DVA: static acuity first; flex 30° and oscillate at 2 Hz, 10–20° each way; a drop of ≥ 3 lines = VOR impairment. 1
08Explain Weber and Rinne testing and interpretation.
Weber: 256/512 Hz fork on the vertex; normal = midline; lateralization = ipsilateral conductive or contralateral sensorineural loss. Rinne: fork on the mastoid, then 2.5 cm from the ear; normal = air louder than bone; bone louder/longer = conductive hearing loss. 1
09Compare spasticity and rigidity, and describe how to test and document tone.
Spasticity: velocity/amplitude-dependent, catch or clasp-knife, often unequal in agonist/antagonist, pyramidal (corticoreticulospinal). Rigidity: constant through ROM, equal in both (lead pipe, cogwheel), extrapyramidal/basal ganglia. Test supported supine; slow stretch for tone, quick stretch from the shortened range for spasticity; less-affected side first; grade with MAS; document the muscle group. 1, 23
10Describe the UMCT: purpose, test parts and how flexion is graded.
Tests voluntary control of the hemiparetic LE in upright; indicated for CNS lesions with patterned movement or spasticity. Flexion control (hip, knee, ankle DF) reflects swing; extension control (hip, knee, ankle PF) reflects stance. Two practice trials, one graded trial. Hip/knee flexion: Weak < 30° or > 10 s for 3 reps; Moderate 30–60° ×3 in 10 s; Strong > 60° ×3 in 10 s. DF has no Moderate grade. 1, 27
11List the UE flexion and extension synergy components and their strongest parts.
Flexion: scapular elevation/retraction, shoulder abduction/ER, elbow flexion (strongest), forearm supination, wrist and finger flexion. Extension: scapular protraction, shoulder adduction and IR, elbow extension, forearm pronation (adduction and pronation strongest), wrist and finger flexion. 1, 24
12Differentiate aphasia, apraxia of speech and dysarthria.
Aphasia: acquired disruption of language (reading, writing, listening, speaking), typically left hemisphere; fluent or non-fluent. Apraxia of speech: motor planning deficit; inconsistent errors, groping, high awareness; not from weakness. Dysarthria: motor execution deficit from weakness, tone or incoordination; slurred; consistent errors; affects respiration, phonation, articulation, prosody, resonance. 1
13List dysphagia warning signs and when you would refer to SLP.
Warning signs: coughing/throat clearing, wet voice after swallowing, fevers after eating/drinking, congestion or wheezing after intake, drooling, unintentional weight loss, long meals, recurrent pneumonia, regurgitation, food sticking, fear of eating or pills. Remember ~50% aspirate silently. Refer with a known neuro diagnosis, signs of aspiration, excessive throat clearing, or unexpected cognitive-communication behavior. 1
14Describe four PNF techniques with their goal and verbal cue.
Rhythmic initiation (PROM → AAROM → AROM → resisted → I; initiation, teach the pattern; 'relax and let me move you… now move with me'). Dynamic reversals (alternating concentric, no relaxation; mobility, strength; 'push against me'). Rhythmic stabilization (alternating isometrics; stability; 'don't let me move you'). Combination of isotonics (concentric-isometric-eccentric; controlled mobility; 'push, hold, let me move you slowly'). Replication (start at the end; teach the final position). Repeated stretch (3–4 per pattern). 1
15Give HIIT dosing after stroke using FITT.
F: 2–5 days/week for 2–4 weeks. I: fastest safe walking speed, or 85–95% HRR, or power at 90–100% VO2peak. T: 25–30 min; burst:recovery 30 s:30–60 s up to 3 min:4 min. T: treadmill if safe or recumbent stepper, monitoring for hypotension. 35
16Describe the four phases of sit to stand with the key muscle activity.
I Flexion-momentum (initiation to just before lift-off): iliopsoas, abs, tibialis anterior concentric; erector spinae eccentric. II Momentum-transfer (lift-off to max DF): hip and knee extensors; highest GRF at lift-off. III Extension (max DF until hips stop extending): co-activation of hip/knee extensors, abdominals. IV Stabilization: co-contraction for upright. 18, 51
17How would you use bridging for both function and strengthening? Include at least two parameter changes.
Bridging feeds tasks like pulling up pants and scooting. Strengthen with combination of isotonics (resist the lift, hold, lower slowly against your push) or rhythmic stabilization in the hold. Parameters: feet closer to the pelvis reduces hamstring activity (and cramps); heel lift or ankle DF increases glute max; single-leg bridge increases trunk co-activation, more with hip abduction; unstable surfaces or vibration add challenge. 1, 48, 49
18Outline your approach to a hypotonic hemiplegic UE in the first week after stroke.
Early mobility in the neuroplasticity window; prevent shoulder complications, contractures and learned non-use. Positioning and handling (support the shoulder in bed and chair), ROM respecting scapulohumeral rhythm (no ROM > 90° unless facilitating upward rotation and ER; max 140°), weight bearing with facilitation, strengthening from the less-involved UE → bilateral → involved. Work the hand early. Consider FES for subluxation; slings are likely not beneficial. 1, 61
19What does the evidence say about strengthening a spastic limb, and what are appropriate goals?
Weakness affects function more than spasticity; resistance training improves strength, gait speed, function and QOL without exacerbating spasticity, and may benefit spasticity. Goals must be patient-centered and functional, never “decrease spasticity”. Treat spasticity when it interferes with function, is painful, or causes contracture or skin breakdown. 62, 63, 64, 65
20What belongs in each part of a neuro treatment SOAP note?
S: what the patient says at the start. O: chronological; vitals with reasons; movement analysis; task, MC, VC, direction of force, reps, assist, setup, equipment, outcome; show skilled PT and progress. A: professional judgment of progress, factors helping or interfering, not a summary of O (safety, fall risk, caregiver burden). P: what you'll do next visit. 1
