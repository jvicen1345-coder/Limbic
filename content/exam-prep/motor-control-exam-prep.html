Topic 1 · Motor Control Theories & Task-Oriented Approach

1AThe 3 movement effectors (constraints), with examples
Break it down

The gistMovement is the product of three interacting constraints: who is moving (individual), what they're doing (task) and where they're doing it (environment). Change any one and the movement changes.

Watch forOpen/closed and discrete/continuous classify the task. Regulatory/non-regulatory classify the environment.

Movement emerges from the interaction of the individual, the task and the environment. 1
  • Individual (resources & constraints): sensory/perceptual, cognitive, and motor/action systems. Examples of constraints: sensibility deficits, weakness, lack of isolated movement, cognitive problems like inability to problem-solve.
  • Task: classified as closed vs open and discrete vs continuous (serial tasks are a third type). Example: a sit-to-stand in a quiet room is closed; hitting a pitched ball is open.
  • Environment: regulatory vs non-regulatory features. Example: a child hitting with many different bats is dealing with a regulatory feature, and a crowd watching is non-regulatory. 2, 3
1BEach theory: key features, limitations, examples
Break it down

The gistThe theories shift from CNS-driven control (reflex, hierarchy, motor programs) toward distributed, emergent control (systems, dynamic action, ecological, NGS). For each one, know the theorist, the core claim, the clinical use and what it can't explain.

Watch forLimitations are the high-yield piece. Novelty trips up reflex and motor program theories, bottom-up control trips up hierarchy, and too little CNS is the complaint against systems, dynamic action and ecological.

Full side-by-side comparison is in 02 Theories. Quick anchors:
  • Reflex (Sherrington): reflexes chained into complex behavior. Fails on movement without a stimulus, fast sequences and novel movements.
  • Reflex-Hierarchical (Jackson, Magnus, Schaltenbrand; Gesell & McGraw): top-down control. Fails on bottom-up dominance (stepping on a tack).
  • Motor Program (Schmidt GMP, CPGs): stored rules let you move without feedback. Fails on storage, novelty and motor equivalence.
  • Systems (Bernstein)4: mastering redundant degrees of freedom. Its weakness is giving the CNS a smaller role.
  • Neuronal Group Selection (Edelman): experience selects neuronal groups. Weakness: controversy over its evolutionary roots.
  • Dynamic Action: self-organization, attractor states, control parameters. Weakness: less emphasis on the CNS.
  • Ecological (Gibson): perception guides action; affordances. Weakness: de-emphasizes the CNS and lacks specificity.
1C-1TOA: the 4 components of assessment and intervention
Break it down

The gistAssess and treat at four mirrored levels, from the person's life down to body structure: participation ↔ foster participation, function ↔ enhance function, strategy ↔ promote strategies, impairment ↔ enhance resources.

Watch forStrategy is how the task is done. Function is whether it gets done.

The approach's assumptions are that normal movement emerges from interacting systems, movement is organized around a goal and constrained by the environment, atypical movement may reveal compensations, and the rehab goal is to foster efficient strategies. It requires sensitive outcome measures to identify key problems, then targeted treatment. 1
  • Assessment: Participation · Function · Strategy (and key components) · Impairment
  • Intervention: Foster participation · Enhance function · Promote strategies · Enhance resources (treat impairments)

1DDiscern: discrete/continuous, regulatory/non-regulatory, open/closed
Break it down

The gistOpen vs closed hinges on whether the environment demands prediction of timing or location. Regulatory features are whatever the movement must conform to.

Watch forSomething can be in the environment and still be non-regulatory if it doesn't shape the movement, like the crowd.

  • Closed: the environment is stable and no prediction is required. Open: timing constraints; you have to predict the speed or location of moving objects, people or surfaces. 2, 3
  • Regulatory features are the features of the environment that the movement must conform to, like the bat, a step's height or the ball's speed. Non-regulatory features don't shape the movement, like a crowd watching. 2, 3
  • Discrete tasks have a recognizable beginning and end: kicking a ball, moving from sitting to standing, lying down. In continuous tasks like walking or running, the end point isn't inherent to the task; the performer decides it (Schmidt 1988). 1 A serial example is making a PB&J sandwich, a series of steps practiced as parts, then as a whole.
  • The textbook's full task scheme (Table 1.1) adds stability vs mobility (a nonmoving base of support, like sitting or standing, vs a moving one, like walking) and manipulation vs nonmanipulation (manipulation tasks involve the upper extremities). 1

Topic 2 · Information Processing, Attention, Motivation & Perception

2AComponents: stimulus identification, response selection, response programming5
Break it down

The gistThree stages sit between sensing and moving: identify the stimulus, select a response, then program the movement. Each stage has its own reaction-time factors.

Watch forHick's law and S-R compatibility belong to response selection. The number of parts, accuracy and duration belong to response programming.

The chain runs from input (vision, vestibular, proprioception, tactile) through processing within the individual to output (the motor response). Reaction time spans all three stages. 1
  • Stimulus identification: stimulus detection (scanning, clarity, intensity), which discovers the stimulus's properties, plus pattern recognition (experience, memory, pairing info), which interprets the stimulus. That interpretation is perception.
  • Response selection: choosing the motor response based on the stimulus's sensory characteristics. Reaction time rises with the number of stimulus-response alternatives (Hick's law), and it is slower when the stimulus and response are incompatible.
  • Response programming: the time to initiate a response increases with the # of moving parts, required accuracy, and movement duration.
  • Related: anticipation can be spatial (foresight of where) or temporal (foresight of when). Prior information lets the response be synchronized to the stimulus. Wickens' expanded model adds working memory and long-term memory.
2B-1Types of attention: focused, sustained, selective, alternating, divided
Break it down

The gistAttention has limited capacity, and the types differ in what you do with it: choose what to process (selective), hold it against distraction (focused), or split it (divided).

Watch forAn external focus beats an internal one for learning (Wulf 1998)6. This comes back in OPTIMAL7 and the running study.

Attention is conscious or non-conscious engagement in perceptual, cognitive and/or motor activities before, during and after performing skills. We can only do a limited number of activities at once. 1
  • Selective: choosing what to process, driven by salience, effort, expectancy and value. It can be intentional (voluntary) or involuntary (automatic, like reacting to a loud sound).
  • Focused: sustaining processing of elements while avoiding distraction.
  • Divided: processing more than one attribute or element in the environment at a time.
  • Sustained attention (vigilance): maintaining a consistent response during continuous, repetitive activity. Alternating attention: the mental flexibility to shift focus and move between tasks with different cognitive requirements. 1
  • Heads up: the textbook's clinical subtypes (Sohlberg & Mateer) swap two definitions compared with the information-processing literature. One framework calls resisting distraction focused attention; the clinical framework calls it selective. State which framework you are using. The textbook's versions: Focused: responding discretely to specific visual, auditory or tactile stimuli. Selective: maintaining a set despite distracting or competing stimuli ("freedom from distractibility"). Divided: responding to multiple tasks or demands at once. Tests: the Random Letter Test (sustained), the Stroop test (selective), Trail-Making A & B (alternating). 1
  • Related: external focus (outside the body) beats internal focus (your own body movement) for motor performance and learning (Wulf 1998)6. Automatic processing is fast, parallel, not attention-demanding and non-volitional. Controlled processing is slow, serial, attention-demanding and volitional.
2B-2Attention theories: single channel, central resource capacity, multiple resource
Break it down

The gistOnly the premise is covered here: attention is limited, so the processing system can handle only so many activities at once. The three named theories need a separate source.

Watch forDon't guess definitions from the names alone.

not covered here The three theories are not defined in the cited sources.
  • What the cited sources do say: the processing system limits how many activities can be done at once. The textbook adds that attention is limited, complex and multidimensional. It can be exogenous (bottom-up, driven by a salient stimulus) or endogenous (top-down, goal-oriented), and it is closely tied to working memory. 1
  • These three theories need a separate source for their definitions.
2B-3Task interference: Stroop, cocktail party, dual task
Break it down

The gistEach paradigm shows a different bottleneck. Stroop is response competition at selection, the cocktail party effect is selective filtering, and dual-tasking is limited capacity, where the cost grows with the complexity of the second task.

Watch forIn the Yogev walking study, controls walked faster than people with PD, and both groups slowed as the second task got harder.

1
  • Stroop effect: identifying the stimuli (the color green and the word "red") happens in parallel, but response selection is delayed by response competition.
  • Cocktail party: when more than one auditory stimulus is present, we choose to ignore one and attend to another.
  • Dual task: walking while doing a second task (Yogev 20058, Parkinson's). The conditions were a simple task (listening to text), a complex task (listening and counting certain words), and serial 7s from 500. Controls walked faster than the PD group, and both groups slowed as the second task got harder.
2CPerception: sensation vs perception; body schema, unilateral neglect, spatial relationship disorder
Break it down

The gistSensation is the signal and perception is its meaning. The perceptual disorders affect the body's internal map (body schema), one side of it (neglect), or its relation to space (spatial relations).

Watch forIn pusher syndrome, vision is normal. The internal sense of upright (posterolateral thalamus) is what's off.

1
  • Sensation is the incoming sensory signal. Perception is the psychological process that relates past experience, memory and judgment to make sensations meaningful. It interprets the sensation during stimulus identification and influences response selection. Perception is an active process that prepares movement and is closely tied to emotion. Perceptual learning means extracting more information and making finer distinctions.
  • Body schema: the internal model of the body (limb position, the body's relationship to itself and the environment), used for spatial organization of action and updated during movement.
  • Unilateral neglect: lack of perception of one side of the body; inability to integrate sensory input from the affected side.
  • Spatial relationship disorder: inability to perceive the relationship between body and environment. Subtypes: topographical (can't remember the relationship between 2 places), figure-ground (can't distinguish foreground from background), position in space (can't determine where you are in relation to gravity).
  • Clinical example, pusher syndrome: the patient actively pushes from the non-paretic side toward the paretic side. Caused by damage to the posterolateral thalamus, with normal visual perception. Patients feel erect but see that they are tilted (Karnath 2000, 2003)9, 10.
  • When perception is impaired, the person can't prepare for movement, judge whether it succeeded, or anticipate its timing. Distinguish a perceptual problem from a movement problem.
2DMotivation: meaning, influences, goal orientation, self-perception of capabilities
Break it down

The gistMotivation drives engagement, and the meaning a person attaches to a task sets their effort and persistence. Mastery-oriented (SRA) learners persist after failure; comparison-oriented (SCA) learners withdraw.

Watch forSelf-efficacy is 'I can do it'; outcome expectation is 'it will work'. Performance accomplishments are the strongest source of self-efficacy.

1
  • Motivation is a critical force or energy that drives a person to engage or sustain involvement in a task. Meaning is dynamic and can change minute to minute: the value someone attaches to a situation determines their choices, effort and persistence. It comes from personal factors (self-perception, confidence, prior experience) or social influences (environmental features, peer support, urgency of others, familiarity).
  • Goal orientation can be mastery (learning), performance (demonstrating competency) or avoidance (fear of failure).
  • Self-referenced achievement (SRA) is mastery-oriented: judged against personal goals ("how did I do before?"). These people choose challenging tasks and self-monitor. After failure they put in more effort and persistence and preserve their problem-solving.
  • Social comparison-based achievement (SCA) uses judgment or ego goals ("how do I do compared to others?"). These people choose either very easy or very difficult tasks. After failure they withdraw effort and show anxiety, frustration and task aversion. Competitive task structures push people toward SCA. Wording matters: "you improved since last time" (SRA) vs "compared to others you're very good" (SCA).
  • Self-efficacy: belief in your capability to perform the specific actions that lead to outcomes. Outcome expectations: belief in the outcomes that certain actions will produce.
  • Sources of self-efficacy: personal performance accomplishments (most powerful), vicarious experience, verbal persuasion, and physiological (arousal) states. Clinically, build a series of efficacy accomplishments, use vicarious experience, use credible persuasion, and give beneficial labeling of sensations ("mild soreness means you're getting stronger"). Assessing self-efficacy before and after treatment shows who needs a boost.

Topic 3 · Motor Learning: Skill Acquisition & Feedback

3ASimilarities: Cognitive stage (Fitts & Posner)11, Early learning (Gentile), Novice (Newell/Bernstein)4. Qualitative or quantitative feedback?
Break it down

The gistAll three 'first stages' describe the same learner: effortful, variable, still figuring out the goal, and stiff from freezing DOF.

Watch forEarly learners get qualitative feedback. Quantitative feedback is for refining later.

  • All three describe a learner who is figuring out the task. Attention demand is high (conscious involvement). The learner must understand the goal and "get in the ballpark" by trying different strategies. They are identifying regulatory features. Performance is highly variable and improvement is rapid.
  • The novice stage adds that the learner freezes degrees of freedom through co-contraction and coupling joints, which makes movement less efficient and less flexible.
  • They benefit from a demonstration for the big picture, verbal clarification of the goal, and verbal cues about factors of success.
  • Feedback: qualitative. Early learners may benefit more from qualitative feedback to help them "get in the ballpark". Quantitative feedback (numbers) helps refine movement later.
3BHow can the power law of practice help you learn to juggle?
Break it down

The gistGains are steep early and flatten as skill rises, but they keep coming with more practice, even over years.

Watch forA plateau is not the end of learning.

Early practice brings rapid skill acquisition, and gains slow as your skill level rises. Improvement is directly related to the amount of practice and can continue for many years (the cigar-rolling study). For juggling, expect big gains in the first sessions and then a flattening curve. A slowdown doesn't mean you've stopped learning; keep logging practice because improvement continues. 2, 3
3CVariable vs random practice; an example of massed practice
Break it down

The gistVariable practice runs one task across a range of conditions (constant practice uses one), which improves transfer to new variations. Random practice mixes the order of different tasks. Massed practice has more practice time than rest.

Watch forBlocked wins performance and random wins learning. The exceptions: complex early-stage skills, PD, children and Alzheimer's.

  • Variable vs constant practice: variable practice means practicing a task across a range of conditions; constant practice uses only one. In the textbook's example, one group practiced a timing task at 5, 7, 9 and 11 mph and another at a single speed. On a new speed outside that range, the variable group made smaller errors (Catalano & Kleiner 1984). Variable practice improves the ability to adapt and generalize to novel variations. It matters most for tasks performed in variable conditions; tasks done in constant conditions may be best practiced constantly. 1 It also improves the accuracy of the recall and recognition schemas. 2
  • Random practice is about the order in which different tasks are practiced (contextual interference): RCICRIRCIC instead of blocked RRRRRCCCCCIIIII. Blocked practice is better for performance and random is better for learning (Shea & Morgan 1979)12. Random practice may be inappropriate for complex skills before the learner understands the task's dynamics, and blocked may be better for retention in PD and in children. People with Alzheimer's didn't benefit from random practice. 2, 3
  • Massed practice means more practice time in a trial than rest between trials; distributed practice means rest is equal to or greater than trial time. For continuous tasks, massed practice lowers performance while it lasts but barely affects learning; for discrete tasks the results depend on the task. Watch for fatigue and fall risk. 1 Examples of massed practice: CIMT / forced use.
3DAn open task from your life and from the clinic
Break it down

The gistAn open task means the environment moves, so you have to predict timing or location.

Watch forClosed doesn't mean easy. It means stable, with no prediction needed.

An open task has timing constraints and requires predicting the speed or location of moving objects, people or surfaces. 2, 3
  • example Life: spotting a client who's moving through a set, or catching a ball a friend throws.
  • example Clinic: gait training in a busy hallway with people crossing, or catching and returning a ball tossed at varying speeds during standing balance work.
3EDescribe an affordance. How can we use it clinically?
Break it down

The gistAn affordance is the fit between person and environment. Change the environment and you change which actions it invites. Ecological plasticity builds on this: more varied, open settings help skills transfer.

Watch forAffordances come from Gibson's ecological theory (Topic 1) and reappear as an environmental-level practice tool (Topic 3).

An affordance is the reciprocal fit between person and environment needed to perform a task (Gibson 1979). Clinically, the person must perceive and adapt to critical aspects of the environment (the "affordances-ergonomics" that shape how movement is organized). A related idea is ecological plasticity: structure the clinic to create variability and more open environments so skills transfer to the real world. 1, 2, 3
example Adjust seat height so the chair affords a sit-to-stand, or size objects to afford a particular grasp.
3FBenefit of a coping model? When to use an expert model?
Break it down

The gistExpert models show the ideal pattern and strategy. Coping and peer models push the learner to problem-solve and show them error correction.

Watch forResearch favors a skilled model overall. The coping model's benefit is engagement and seeing error correction.

  • Coping model: someone with imperfect skill, like an older sibling. Beginners who watch beginners perform better than the person they observed. They don't want to imitate an imperfect performance, so they engage more in solving the motor problem themselves, and they learn from watching the model correct errors.
  • Expert model (coach, therapist or adult): provides important strategies. Research suggests watching a skilled model is best overall. Use it before practice to convey the goal, the invariant features of the pattern and the strategy (Gentile's first stage). Videos or live demos beat still pictures.
  • A peer model has similar characteristics to the learner.

3GGuidelines for manual guidance. What should you be cautious of?
Break it down

The gistUse guidance briefly to convey the goal or to restrict what the learner must control, then fade it.

Watch forThe risk is that guidance becomes a regulatory feature, so the patient depends on it.

  • Manual guidance is different from traditional manual therapy.
  • Passive movement (1–2×): position the limb to allow movement, or move it to build understanding of the goal or spatial features.
  • Physical restriction: hold part of the limb stable, or constrain it so the person has only one part to control.
  • Caution: guidance can quickly become one of the regulatory features of the task, so the patient comes to depend on it. Remove it as quickly as possible.

3HWhat is "bandwidth of error"? Clinical example
Break it down

The gistGive feedback only when error exceeds a set range. That means fewer, more meaningful corrections and more reliance on the learner's own intrinsic feedback.

Watch forThis is one of the three ways to reduce feedback frequency. The others are self-selected and summary or averaged feedback.

A performance bandwidth means you only give feedback once error exceeds a set amount (for example, bowling: no feedback while within the 20% bandwidth). Error information is more effective for learning than correctness information. A bandwidth also reduces how often you give feedback, which keeps the learner using their own intrinsic feedback. 2, 3
example During squats, cue only when a knee drifts noticeably past the second toe, and stay quiet on reps inside that range.
3IWhen does concurrent feedback aid learning? When is it detrimental?
Break it down

The gistFeedback during the task helps performance now but can create dependence. Less frequent, faded feedback builds lasting learning.

Watch forThe negative effect of concurrent feedback is the more common one.

Concurrent feedback is given during the task, and its effects vary. It enhances learning when it helps the learner identify their own intrinsic feedback. The negative effect is more common: acquisition (performance) improves but learning doesn't, because the learner becomes dependent on it. Prefer intermittent feedback (less than 100% frequency is best for learning; it fosters active engagement and prevents dependence) and faded feedback (gradually decreasing). 2, 3
3JA knowledge of performance (KP) example
Break it down

The gistKP is about how you moved. KR is about what happened.

Watch forEither can be intrinsic or extrinsic. Classify by content, not by who gives it.

KP is information about the movement features that led to the outcome, and ways to improve. It is best for complex movement tasks. KR (outcome) confirms intrinsic feedback, motivates, and helps when the learner can't see the outcome. 2, 3
example Extrinsic KP: "Your trunk pitched forward and your heels came up as you stood from the chair." Intrinsic KP: "I felt my hips shoot back before my chest came up."
3KIndicators of learning
Break it down

The gistLearning shows up as retention and transfer, plus consistency, flexibility and efficiency, never just as in-session performance.

Watch forPerformance during practice can mislead: blocked practice and concurrent feedback both inflate it.

Retention (the same task after a time delay) and transfer (the task with slight variation in timing, force or movement pattern) are the best indicators. Others: efficiency, flexibility, consistency. 2, 3

Topic 4 · Motor Learning Theories

4APerformance vs learning; ways to measure
Break it down

The gistPerformance is what you see today; learning is what lasts. Test learning after a delay (retention) or on a variation (transfer).

Watch forAveraged performance curves hide temporary changes. Watch for ceiling and floor effects.

  • Performance is the output observed during practice. It's a temporary fluctuation, influenced by fatigue, anxiety, motivation, arousal, confidence and mental preparation.
  • Learning is a relatively permanent change in behavior that isn't directly observable. It results from experience or practice, and must be inferred from motor behavior over time.
  • Ways to measure: performance curves (change over time; averaging cancels out variation and masks temporary changes; watch for ceiling and floor effects), retention tests, transfer tests, and efficiency, flexibility and consistency.

Break it down

The gistRecovery restores the original way of doing something; compensation finds another way. Both are described at three levels: neural tissue, body function and activity.

Watch forDirect recovery at the tissue level means temporarily suppressed tissue coming back online (after inflammation, edema or inhibition).

Recovery achieves function through the original neural processes. Compensation achieves function through alternative processes or behavioral substitution. 2

4CMemory & learning: all types
Break it down

The gistWorking memory holds information briefly; long-term memory stores it structurally. Explicit memory is conscious recall of facts; implicit memory runs without awareness. Implicit memory splits into non-associative (one stimulus), associative (linking stimuli or behavior with consequences) and procedural (skills).

Watch forExplicit cues can interfere with implicit learning, especially after a basal ganglia stroke.

2
  • Working (short-term) memory: storage lasts 1–60 s, abstract coding, capacity limited to momentary attention. Long-term memory: limitless duration and capacity, very abstract coding, structural changes at the synapse, less susceptible to disruption.
  • Explicit (declarative): high awareness and attention; conscious, intentional recall of facts, concepts and experiences; held in working memory; relies on cognitive processes (mental rehearsal, evaluation, comparison, inference). Implicit (procedural): no awareness or cognition needed and hard to express verbally; automatic and habitual; accumulates with repetition, so learning is slow.
  • Non-associative learning. Habituation is a suppressed response, like a decreased response to tickling or less dizziness with repeated stimulation. Tx: repeated vestibular exercises reduce EPSP size and the number of responding synapses. Sensitization strengthens the response to a noxious stimulus. Negative form: a lowered pain threshold. Positive Tx: meditation to raise the pain threshold, or heightened awareness of vestibular cues that you're going off balance.
  • Associative learning: the learner predicts a relationship. Classical conditioning pairs 2 stimuli: a weak conditioned stimulus (bell) comes to produce the response linked to a strong unconditioned one (food). Operant conditioning is contingent reinforcement: rewarded behaviors tend to be repeated. Key areas: cerebellum, amygdala, premotor area.
  • Procedural learning: comes from repeated, varied practice, as in mastering a sequential motor task. The skilled performer exploits external forces, minimizes active muscle force and moves smoothly (Bernstein)4. Co-contraction is replaced by efficient activation as prediction improves. It requires no awareness, and the striatum of the basal ganglia is critical.
  • Interference: explicit cues during high-attention tasks, and extra effort to remember rules, disrupt implicit learning. After a basal ganglia stroke, explicit cues can make implicit motor-sequence learning harder.
  • Clinically, explicit teaching means directing attention to body-environment relationships and key movement markers, chunking, memory cues and fewer distractions. Implicit teaching means repeated practice, a wide range of speed and force, a focus on the goal before movement quality, and accepting error.

5AShort-term vs long-term plasticity. Which is linked with implicit learning?
Break it down

The gistShort-term plasticity means existing synapses work better (a functional change). Long-term plasticity means more synapses (a structural change).

Watch forThe sources don't answer the implicit-learning part directly. Inference: implicit memory is a type of long-term memory (structural), but habituation and sensitization have short-term forms too.

Short-term plasticity is a functional change in how existing synapses work (the diagram labels it a change in synaptic efficacy): faster conduction, more efficient. The pre- and post-synaptic cells "talk more". Long-term plasticity is a structural change: the number of connections among neurons increases, as in dendritic branching (a growth cone with calcium). The two form a parallel continuum where persisting short-term changes become long-term changes. 2 Which one goes with implicit learning? inference The sources don't state this as a one-line answer, but the framing is clear. Implicit (nondeclarative) learning is a form of long-term memory, and long-term memory is itself a continuum: early stages are functional changes in synaptic efficiency, later stages are structural changes in connections. Habituation, "the simplest form of implicit learning", starts as a short-term EPSP decrease lasting minutes, and with repetition becomes persisting change, a longer-term memory. 1 So the strongest answer is long-term, built on short-term changes that persist with practice. 2
5BDescribe long-term potentiation
Break it down

The gistLTP is a synapse getting stronger: the same input produces a bigger EPSP. The early phase is functional (transmitter release); the later phase is structural (new synapses).

Watch forIt requires simultaneous pre- and post-synaptic firing, the same rule as Hebbian and associative learning.

LTP is the strengthening of synapses. 2
  • A first weak stimulus produces a small EPSP (excitatory post-synaptic potential). After LTP, the same-size stimulus produces a large EPSP. That's what the graph shows.
  • LTP requires simultaneous firing of the pre- and post-synaptic cells, which ties it to Hebbian and associative learning.
  • Early (short-term) phase, 1–3 hours: a functional change. The presynaptic cell changes its transmitter release and reuptake slows, so glutamate "hangs out" in the synapse and has a better chance to bind.
  • Later phase, about 24 hours: structural changes, meaning new synapses and release sites form (new NMDA-dependent synapses).
  • From the textbook: LTP is a facilitation first shown in the hippocampus, similar to the mechanism of sensitization (Bliss & Lomo 1973)14. A weak input is enhanced when it arrives together with a strong input on the same dendrite. It is maintained through increased presynaptic transmitter release. The early phase (1–3 h) needs no protein synthesis; the late phase (≥24 h) requires cAMP/CREB-mediated gene expression and protein synthesis and adds new synaptic release sites. Its opposite, long-term depression (LTD), weakens a connection when the postsynaptic action potential precedes the presynaptic one. Both are shaped by neuromodulators (dopamine, noradrenaline, acetylcholine, serotonin) and GABA inhibition. 1
5CHabituation or sensitization: which increases connections? The short-term process of each
Break it down

The gistHabituation turns a response down, with fewer connections long-term. Sensitization turns it up, with more connections.

Watch forThe short-term mechanisms differ: habituation decreases the EPSP, while sensitization modulates K⁺ and Ca²⁺ to prolong the AP.

Sensitization increases the number of connections. Long-term habituation decreases them. 2
  • Habituation, short-term: a decreased EPSP produced by the sensory neuron on the interneuron and motor neuron.
  • Sensitization, short-term: modulation of K⁺ channel conduction and the amount of intracellular Ca²⁺. This prolongs AP duration, causing greater neurotransmitter release and EPSP amplitude.
5DHow is associative learning linked to Hebbian learning?
Break it down

The gistHebb's rule: neurons that fire together wire together. It underlies associative learning and LTP.

Watch forIn associative learning, the learner predicts the relationship.

In associative learning (classical and operant conditioning), the learner predicts the relationship. Two neurons active at the same time modify existing proteins to change synaptic efficacy. That is Hebb's rule, summarized as "nerve cells that fire together wire together" (Hebb 1949)15. 2

5HInfant with a right M1 stroke reaches well with both arms at 5 months, but struggles with the left at 8 months. Why?
Break it down

The gistEarly on, the intact M1 also reaches the affected arm and masks the injury. When pruning removes those pathways, the weakness shows up once a skill like reaching demands it.

Watch forThe right M1 injury shows up in the left arm, and it's pruning of the intact side's pathways that reveals it.

Selective pruning. After a unilateral M1 injury, pruning of pathways from the intact M1 reveals outward signs of hemiplegia with the onset of reaching. 5ITwo phenomena that depress brain function early after injury. Which is temporary?
Break it down

5MOne form of activity-based therapy. When should therapy start post-stroke?
Break it down

The gistPlasticity is driven by experience, but the right timing and dose after stroke are still debated.

Watch forKnow which trial goes with what: EXCITE23 (CIMT at 3–9 months) and VECTORS24 (dosage).

  • The post-stroke brain is primed for this: repair-related changes in both the stroke and non-stroke hemispheres ("stage is set", Cramer 2008). Inflammatory markers, growth-associated proteins, cell-cycle proteins, growth factors, NMDA receptor binding, angiogenesis, synaptogenesis, dendritic branching, neuronal sprouting and cortical thickness all increase. GABA is downregulated early, and hyperexcitability drives LTP.

The overarching principle is that experience is key to drive plasticity.

The effect of intensity may depend on timing. 1
  • no single answer The PD findings come from animal models, and timing is still called "a big debate" (with concern about exacerbating injury). A fair summary: evidence favors early activity-based therapy, with intensity matched to timing.

5OHow exercise type and practice features drive plasticity
Break it down

The gistDifferent exercise changes different tissue. Skill training changes cortical maps, strength training changes the spinal cord, and endurance training changes the cortex's blood supply (plus BDNF with HIIT).

Watch forOnly skill training changes the motor map.

2
  • Skill training: induces synaptogenesis, synaptic potentiation, myelinization, and reorganization of representations within the motor cortex.
  • Strength training: alters spinal motoneuron excitability and induces synaptogenesis within the spinal cord. It does not change the motor map.
  • Endurance (cardiopulmonary) training: induces angiogenesis in the motor cortex, but does not change the motor map or the number of synapses. HIIT (raising heart rate; Lara Boyd, PT, PhD) increases BDNF, primes LTP-like neuroplasticity, and promotes sequence-specific implicit learning.

5PNeural growth, neural injury & reorganization details
Break it down

The gistThe nervous system is built in five steps, injury disrupts its connections, and recovery is best when areas of similar function take over.

Watch forRemember the order: proliferation → migration → synaptogenesis → pruning → myelination.

2
  • Neuroplasticity is the ability of the brain and nervous system to form and reorganize neural connections, particularly in response to learning, experience or injury. It involves both structural and functional adaptation.
  • Neural growth (Volpe 2008)27: proliferation (an increase in cell number through growth and division), migration (neurons move from where they're generated to their final location), synaptogenesis (new connections and networks form), selective pruning (removal of redundant or less-used connections and neurons), and myelinization (adding a fatty layer that speeds conduction).
  • Neuronal function after injury is affected by: interruption of axon projections, denervation of neurons by injured neurons, and removal of neurons. The cascade creates a new (re)learning environment, which rehab should promote.
  • Reorganization of the affected hemisphere: after M1 damage, other cortical areas in the same hemisphere activate through redundant pathways, or new regions take over. The best recovery comes with more normal activation patterns, where areas of similar function take over. When areas not designed for the function take over, optimal recovery isn't seen.
  • Cerebellum: handles motor learning and the formation of automatic motor skills. The cerebellar cortex opposite the damaged CST is most influential, and cerebellar activation increases 2–6 months post-stroke.
  • Structural change from long-term learning: more gray matter in the parietal cortex of mathematicians (Aydin 2007), and in motor, auditory and visual-spatial areas of musicians (Bermudez 2008).
  • Somatosensory cortex damage doesn't disrupt motor skills already learned, but it limits new learning.

Research spotlight · Autonomy and running efficiency

R1Iwatsuki et al.: "Autonomy enhances running efficiency" (Iwatsuki, Navalta & Wulf 2019)28
Break it down

The gistA small choice, even an irrelevant one, made running measurably more efficient. It's autonomy from OPTIMAL7 in action.

Watch forVO₂ and HR were significantly lower, RER was not, and RPE was unchanged.

  • Purpose: test whether autonomy support improves motor performance, using direct metabolic measures of movement efficiency such as oxygen consumption.
  • Hypothesis: giving runners a relatively small choice would improve running efficiency compared with a yoked control group that got no choice.
  • Theory link, OPTIMAL7 (Wulf & Lewthwaite): the three factors are autonomy, enhanced expectancies and external focus. With autonomy, performers keep their focus on the task goal without extra self-regulatory activity or having to suppress negative emotional reactions, and self-efficacy rises. Choice acts as a reward, which releases dopamine, strengthens goal-action coupling and makes the nervous system more efficient. Cortisol downregulates dopamine, and autonomy protects against that. Earlier work showed that both task-relevant and task-irrelevant choices help, such as choosing the golf ball color in a putting task.
  • Participants: 32 college volunteers (16 M, 16 F; mean age 22.6 ± 2.5 years), all low risk on the ACSM questionnaire. The power analysis (G*Power, power .90, α .05) required 22. Participants were told the study was a fitness test, so they didn't know its real purpose. The groups didn't differ at baseline in VO₂max, RERmax or HRmax.
  • Day 1: height and weight, then a treadmill VO₂max test. The speed increased to a self-selected pace, then the grade increased by 3 (units not reported) until exhaustion.
  • Day 2 (one week later): a warm-up, then a 20-minute submaximal run at the speed linked to 65% of VO₂max. The choice group picked 5 of 10 photos to show on a monitor and their order. Each yoked control saw the same photos in the order chosen by a choice-group participant. RER was measured every 30 s, RPE (on a 20-point scale) every 2 min, plus VO₂ and HR.
  • Design and analysis: a quasi-randomized controlled trial. RER, HR and VO₂ were averaged over 5-minute intervals and analyzed with a Group (2) × Time (4) repeated-measures ANOVA; RPE used Group (2) × Time (10). The analysis used Mauchly's sphericity test, with the Greenhouse-Geisser correction if it was violated, and Bonferroni post hoc tests.
  • Results: the choice group ran more efficiently. Oxygen consumption (VO₂) was significantly lower (group effect p < .05), heart rate was significantly lower (group effect p < .05), and RER was lower but not significantly (group p = .93). RPE showed no group difference, only an effect of time (p < .001).
  • Takeaway: even an irrelevant choice enhances movement efficiency. Supporting evidence cited with the study: running to preferred music is more efficient, and self-efficacy is related to motor performance.
  • Clinical application: give patients small choices, like the order of exercises, which equipment to use or what music to play, to support autonomy (OPTIMAL)7.
  • Critique: it's a simple, clear example of OPTIMAL7 theory. Internal validity: a controlled set-up and clear outcome measures (metabolic measures are the best option), but blinding isn't clear, and high inter-rater reliability suggests it could be replicated. External validity: the results apply to college-age runners and can't be generalized to other age groups or clinical populations.

Only using the good arm

01Which theory places the most emphasis on brain development? (A Systems, B Neuronal Darwinism, C Dynamic Action, D Ecological, E Reflex)
B. Neuronal Darwinism (Edelman's Neuronal Group Selection). Its first tenet is brain development: species-specific, with individual diversity from competition for connections, and shaped by the environment. The second is experience increasing interconnections. 1
02T/F: A long-practiced, preferred movement pattern is harder to change because, per Dynamic Systems Theory, it is stable, in a deep attractor well.
True. Attractor states are preferred patterns of movement. Stable patterns have deep attractor wells and unstable ones have shallow wells, so a deep well takes more to push the system out of. 1
03T/F: Adams' Closed Loop Theory includes recall and recognition schemas.
False. Recall and recognition schemas belong to Schmidt's Schema13 Theory. Adams uses a memory trace (selects and initiates movement) and a perceptual trace (the reference of correctness that detects error). 2
04Describe degrees of freedom. How is it seen in clients during early vs late gait practice after neural injury?
The body has many muscles and joints, and a movement can be carried out in multiple equivalent ways. The degrees of freedom problem is choosing among those solutions and coordinating everything involved (Bernstein)4. 1 Bernstein defined coordination as mastering or optimizing redundant DOF, with synergies reducing them.
  • Early practice (novice stage): the client freezes DOF by constraining or coupling joints with co-contraction of agonists and antagonists. Movement is stiff, less efficient and less flexible, and the body can't respond flexibly to the environment. This justifies external support, since assistive devices reduce DOF. example A stiff-kneed, guarded gait holding the parallel bars.
  • Later practice (advanced to expert): the client releases DOF, with more independent joint control and less co-contraction. Eventually they exploit forces like momentum, so gait becomes faster with lower energy cost.
1, 2, 3
05Name 3 theories of motor control and one feature of each.
Any three, for example:
  • Reflex (Sherrington): reflexes chained together form complex behavior.
  • Motor Program: a CNS representation allows movement without sensory feedback, and a GMP holds invariants (sequence, relative timing, relative force).
  • Systems (Bernstein)4: control is distributed; coordination means mastering redundant DOF.
  • Dynamic Action: self-organization with no command center, and attractor states.
  • Ecological (Gibson): perception guides action; affordances.
  • Neuronal Group Selection (Edelman): experience selects neuronal groups; active, self-generated movement is essential.
1
06Dynamic Action Theory: what is a control parameter and an order parameter? Give an example of each.
  • Control parameter: the variable (the treatment method) that regulates change in the behavior of the whole system. Example: velocity or speed, such as treadmill speed.
  • Order parameter: the variable that shows how stable or unstable the behavior pattern is. example The gait pattern itself: as speed passes a critical point, a jog falls out and becomes a run.
1
07Describe Ecological Theory by James Gibson.
Gibson (a psychologist, 1960s) proposed that perception guides action. Sensory input with active perception helps reach motor goals, and action is organized specifically to the task and environment. The person must perceive and adapt to critical aspects of the environment (affordances). Evidence: von Hofsten & Lindhagen 1979, where 18-week-old infants reached only for moving objects they could likely catch, and the visual cliff. Clinically, examine the client's ability to actively explore (scan) and perceive critical features, and emphasize adapting to accomplish the task. Limitations: it de-emphasizes the CNS and MSK systems and lacks specificity for problems like tone. 1
08Describe key features of Adams' theory.
Adams' Closed Loop Theory: a memory trace selects and initiates movement, and a perceptual trace is the internal reference of correctness, built with practice, that detects error. Its key feature is the role of feedback: sensory feedback from the ongoing movement is compared with the stored memory of the intended movement, and the reference is refined. Practice and accurate movement strengthen the perceptual trace. It explains slow, unvaried movement. Limitations: it can't explain novel movements, movements without feedback, or fast movements, and it treats variability as error. 2
09Name 5 of Kleim & Jones' 10 principles of neuroplasticity that clients can use.
All 10: use it or lose it · use it & improve it · specificity · repetition · intensity matters · time matters · salience matters · age matters · transference · interference. 2
10Based on research evidence, name methods used clinically to foster neuroplasticity.
  • Alter dosage (frequency, duration, intensity), give challenging tasks at the ideal timing, and aim for "just the right challenge".
  • Skill, strength and endurance training, including HIIT, which increases BDNF and primes LTP-like plasticity.
  • Activity-based therapy such as CIMT (EXCITE23 trial, 3–9 months post-stroke).
  • Mental plus physical practice (Page 2007)29.
  • Neuromodulation: rTMS or tDCS to inhibit the contralesional hemisphere; transcutaneous spinal cord stimulation.
  • Ecological plasticity: variable, more open environments.
  • Avoid training only the less-affected limb early (Allred 2005)21.

11T/F: Allred & Jones (2005, rats): training the less-affected forelimb after unilateral CVA interferes with recovery of the impaired forelimb.
True. The early-training group showed little improvement in reaching with the affected limb and more disuse of it. This is maladaptive plasticity driven by competitive processes. 2
12Match: A Unmasking, B Collateral sprouting, C Regeneration → intact axons sprout onto sites left by injured axons / firming silent synapses / axons sprout.
A Unmasking → firming silent synapses. B Collateral sprouting → intact axons sprout onto sites left by injured axons (same system). C Regeneration → axons sprout. 2
13Short-term neuroplasticity involves ___ change, whereas long-term involves ___ changes.
Functional (faster, more efficient conduction) vs structural (more connections among neurons, dendritic branching). 2
14What is ecological plasticity and how can PTs use it?
The positive effect of environmental stimulation on brain reorganization. Structure the clinic to create variability and more open environments, so people transfer skills to the real world. example Practice gait on varied surfaces, around moving people or with real-world objects, not just in a quiet, closed gym. 2, 3
15What does aerobic exercise or HIIT do for learning?
Endurance (cardiopulmonary) training induces angiogenesis in the motor cortex, without changing the motor map or synapse number. HIIT (raising heart rate; Lara Boyd) increases BDNF, primes LTP-like neuroplasticity, and promotes sequence-specific implicit learning. 2
16Which is an example of enhanced expectations? (A focus on task outcomes, B choose an item, C tell them people their age do well, D focus on the floor on a balance board)
C. Telling the patient that people in their age bracket typically do well raises their expectancy of success. A is an external focus and B is autonomy. Those are the other two OPTIMAL7 factors. D isn't an OPTIMAL factor; it points attention away from the task goal. 2, 28
17Key differences between explicit and implicit learning?
Explicit (declarative): high awareness and attention, conscious recall of facts, held in working memory, relies on cognitive processes (rehearsal, evaluation, inference). Implicit (procedural): no awareness needed, hard to verbalize, automatic, accumulates slowly with repetition. It includes non-associative (habituation, sensitization), associative (classical, operant) and procedural learning. Clinically, explicit teaching uses cues, chunking and key movement markers, while implicit teaching uses repeated varied practice, a focus on the goal first, and accepting error. Explicit cues can interfere with implicit learning, for example after a basal ganglia stroke. 2
18Describe Gentile's model. How is it similar to and different from Fitts & Posner?
Gentile, 2 stages. Early: understand the goal and task dynamics (such as the force needed), "get in the ballpark" by developing strategies, and distinguish regulatory from non-regulatory features. Later: refine the movement, adapt to changing task and environmental demands, and perform consistently and efficiently. Gentile describes a consistent movement pattern for closed skills vs a diverse one for open skills. Similar: Gentile's early stage matches Fitts & Posner's cognitive phase11: high attention, understanding the goal, identifying regulatory features, variable performance. Both progress toward consistent, efficient performance. Different: Fitts & Posner has 3 phases (cognitive, associative, autonomous) defined by the attention and conscious involvement needed. Its autonomous phase allows dual tasking. Gentile uses 2 stages defined by the learner's goal, with an emphasis on the environment and on closed vs open skills. 2, 3
19T/F: Diaschisis is the transient loss of function of intact tissue from loss of input from damaged tissue, with decreased blood flow or metabolism.
True (Feeney & Baron 1986)20. 2
20The 3 movement effectors or constraints (Shumway-Cook & Woollacott), with an example of each.
Individual (sensory/perceptual, cognitive, motor/action), e.g. weakness, sensibility deficits, or trouble problem-solving. Task (closed/open, discrete/continuous), e.g. catching a moving ball (open) vs sit-to-stand (closed). Environment (regulatory vs non-regulatory), e.g. the bat and ball the swing must conform to vs a crowd watching. 1, 2, 3
21Which describes a psychological process that relates past experience, memory and judgment to sensations? (A Feelings, B Perception, C Sensory mapping, D Learning)
B. Perception. 1
22What is self-efficacy and why does it matter?
Self-efficacy is one's belief in one's capability to perform the specific actions that lead to outcomes, a situation-specific self-confidence. It matters because the OPTIMAL7 theory links enhanced expectancies to dopamine and better learning, and the Iwatsuki et al. study notes a relationship between self-efficacy and motor performance. Sources: performance accomplishments (the most powerful), vicarious experience, verbal persuasion, and physiological states. PTs build it with a series of efficacy accomplishments, vicarious experience, credible persuasion, beneficial labeling of sensations, and pre/post self-efficacy assessments. 1, 28
23What is selective attention and how can it influence treatment success?
Selective attention is the ability to perceive some stimuli and ignore others: choosing what to process, based on salience, effort, expectancy and value. It can be intentional (voluntary) or involuntary (like a loud sound). In treatment, what the client attends to shapes learning. Direct attention to the relevant cues and regulatory features with short cues and an external focus (Wulf 1998)6, and reduce distractions for early learners, since explicit learning needs full attention. Distractions or irrelevant cues pull attention from the task goal. 1, 2, 3
24Why might a learner benefit from a peer or coping model instead of an expert?
Beginners who watch beginners can perform at a higher level than the person they observed. They don't want to imitate an imperfect performance, so they engage more in solving the motor problem themselves, fitted to their own abilities. They also pick up information from watching the model detect and correct errors. A peer has similar characteristics, which also supports self-efficacy through vicarious experience. 1, 2, 3
25A primary implication of Fitts' law, and how to use it clinically.
It's the speed-accuracy tradeoff: a smaller target means longer movement time, and a larger target allows faster movement over the same distance. MT = a + b·log₂(2A/W), where the index of difficulty depends on amplitude and target width. example Grade tasks by target size and distance. Use larger or closer targets when working on speed, and shrink targets when training accuracy. Expect slower movement when accuracy demands go up. 2, 3
26T/F: In Gentile's model, the early stage involves the learner "getting in the ballpark".
True. 2, 3
273 time points to give feedback during a rehab session, with an example of each.
Before the task: demonstration or modeling, or mental practice and imagery. During the task (concurrent): manual guidance, verbal cues, biofeedback. After the task (terminal): KR ("you walked 3 s faster") or KP ("your arm didn't go back far enough"). 2, 3
28Feedback control vs anticipatory (feedforward) control?
Textbook: within the cerebellum, internal feedback signals (the intended movement) are compared with external feedback signals (the actual movement), and the cerebellum generates a corrective signal used in feedforward control to reduce errors in subsequent movements. In a monkey experiment, cooling cerebellar nuclei meant the triceps no longer braked an elbow movement in a feedforward (anticipatory) way; it fired only in a feedback way, after being stretched by the overshoot. Schmidt's schema13 theory emphasized open-loop processes. 1 Feedback (closed-loop) control uses sensory feedback from the ongoing movement to detect and correct error (Adams' theory; suited to slow movements). Anticipatory (feedforward) control prepares and adjusts before or ahead of sensory feedback, based on prediction (open-loop, like motor programs; needed for fast movements). As prediction and feedforward control improve with practice, co-contraction drops. Cerebellar damage causes feedforward deficits, and impaired anticipatory control shows up as inaccurate foot placement on stairs. 1, 2
29Components of the Task-Oriented Approach, with assessment and treatment examples for each.
Participation: assess with a QoL questionnaire; treat with small groups, on-site groups or community programs. Function: assess with Berg, FGI, PEDI, Jebsen-Taylor or the Motor Activity Log; treat by practicing functional tasks in context, CIMT or bilateral training. Strategy: assess by observing how the action is done (e.g., foot placement on stairs); treat with environment set-up, manual guidance, altered task demands, modeling or mental rehearsal. Impairment: assess with MMT, PROM, spasticity or the FACES scale; treat to enhance resources: strength (Theraband), joint mobilization, reducing tone (Botox), sensory re-education.
