Topic 1 · Arthrokinematics, AROM & goniometry

Arthrokinematics · active ROM examination · goniometry and cervical MMT
1ATypical vs atypical cervical vertebrae: what each region does
Break it down

The gistThe upper cervical spine does one job each: C0–1 nods (flexion and extension), and C1–2 turns (rotation). The typical segments, C2–7, do a bit of everything.

Watch forThe 55% figure goes with rotation at C1–2. The 75% figure goes with C2–7 sagittal-plane motion.

  • Typical, C2–7: separated by the intervertebral disc and neural arch. They produce forward/backward bending, side bending and rotation, and account for 75% of motion in the sagittal plane. Key landmarks: lateral mass and articular pillar. 1
  • Atypical, C0–C1: forward/backward bending, with some side flexion.
  • Atypical, C1–C2: rotation is the primary motion, with small amounts of flexion, extension and side flexion (Neumann’s segmental data list no side flexion at C1–2; see 1B). It accounts for 55% of cervical rotation.
1BSegmental arthrokinematics and ROM by region (Neumann 2002)
Break it down

The gistAt C2–7, "opening" means the upper facet glides up and forward (upglide), and "closing" means it glides down and back (downglide). Flexion opens both sides. Extension closes both sides. Rotation and side bending close the side you turn toward (ipsilateral) and open the other side (contralateral).

Watch forAt C0–1, roll and slide go in opposite directions. C0–1 has negligible rotation. For C1–2 side flexion, Neumann lists no motion, while other descriptions allow small amounts. Source conflict

1CAROM exam: instructions, what to observe, clinical pearls
Break it down

The gistFor every motion, look at four things: baseline pain, quantity, quality and symptom reproduction. Quality is where the movement-system findings show up: pure rotation vs translation, and where along the spine the motion happens.

Watch forTwo pearls to know: ipsilateral rotation under 60° is part of the radiculopathy cluster, and flexion/extension limits matter in cervicogenic headache.

General AROM assessment: baseline pain · quantity of movement · quality of movement · symptom reproduction. 2 Note: distinguish a strength problem from tightness.
  • Rotation: "Turn your head as far right/left as possible and let me know if you have pain." Observe segmental rotation, compensations and symptoms. Normal is ≈80°. Pearl: under 60° of rotation to the ipsilateral side is one finding in the cluster that rules in cervical radiculopathy. If rotation is painful, you can do manual-feedback movement re-education to move on axis. Unload the shoulders if needed, and watch for more motion or less pain.
  • Flexion: "Look down as far as you can." Does the head purely rotate forward, or translate? You should see the cervical lordosis reverse, with most movement at C0–1 and C5–7. Normal is 45–50°.
  • Extension: "Look up as far as you can." Watch for too much motion or a backward slide of the head. Normal is 50–80°. Pearl: limitations in flexion and extension are clinically relevant in cervicogenic headaches.
  • Side bending: "Bring your ear toward your shoulder." Normal is 45–50°.
  • Unilateral shoulder flexion: palpate the mid cervical spine with 3–4 fingers. There should be no cervical movement with UE movement. With bilateral shoulder flexion, palpating cervical movement means a worse problem.

1FCervical MMT: the 5 tests and the grading exceptions
Break it down

The gistMost tests use the standard scale: 5 holds against max, 4 against moderate, 3 full ROM with no resistance, 2 partial, 1 palpable contraction, 0 nothing.

Watch forC/S flexion is the exception. Grade 5 holds against moderate 2-finger resistance and grade 4 against mild 2-finger resistance. For unilateral SCM, turn the head right to test the left SCM.

  • Capital extension: rectus capitis posterior major/minor, longissimus capitis, obliquus capitis superior/inferior, splenius capitis, semispinalis capitis, upper trapezius, spinalis capitis, SCM. For grade 1, palpate the occiput muscles.
  • C/S extension: longissimus, semispinalis, iliocostalis, splenius and spinalis cervicis, upper trapezius, multifidi/rotatores, levator scapula. Grade 2 completes partial ROM (pushes into the therapist's hands).
  • Capital flexion: rectus capitis anterior and lateralis, longus capitis, and the suprahyoids (mylohyoid, stylohyoid, geniohyoid, digastric). All grades are shown in one position (grade 0–5).
  • C/S flexion: SCM, longus colli, anterior/middle/posterior scalenes, and the infrahyoids (sternothyroid, thyrohyoid, sternohyoid, omohyoid). 5 = moderate 2-finger, 4 = mild 2-finger resistance; 2 = limited ROM; 1 = palpate the SCM.
  • Unilateral SCM: turn R to test L, turn L to test R. Standard 5/4 grading.

Topic 2 · Screening for serious conditions

Red flags · Canadian C-Spine Rule · instability, vascular, malignancy and myelopathy screening
2AThe 5 serious conditions to screen for, and Grieve's 3 mandatory questions
Break it down

The gistBefore treating the neck, rule out the things that can't wait: a fracture, an unstable upper cervical spine, an artery problem, cancer, or cord compression. Grieve's three questions are a fast screen for the instability, vascular and cord problems.

Watch forEach question maps to a condition: dizziness, blackouts or drop attacks → vascular. RA on systemic steroids → ligamentous instability and fracture risk. Neuro symptoms in the legs → myelopathy. my mapping

  • Screen for: spinal fracture · ligamentous stability · vascular pathologies of the neck · malignancy · cervical myelopathy.
  • Grieve's 3 mandatory C/S questions (ask with all cervical patients): (1) Any dizziness, blackouts or drop attacks? (2) Any history of RA or inflammatory arthritis treated with systemic steroids? (3) Any neurological symptoms in the legs? Note: ask before treatment.
2BSpinal fracture: the Canadian C-Spine Rule, step by step
Break it down

The gistThere are three gates in order. Any high-risk factor → x-ray. Then you need at least one low-risk factor before it's safe to test ROM. Then, if they can actively rotate 45° both ways, no x-ray.

Watch forIt only applies to alert, medically stable patients after acute trauma. It doesn't apply if GCS <15, age <16, the patient is pregnant, and so on. Also think insufficiency fractures in osteoporosis, older adults and long-term corticosteroid use, even with low loads.

Use it for patients with acute blunt head or neck trauma. It's a validated decision rule for whether imaging is indicated in alert, medically stable patients after acute trauma. 4
  • 1. Any high-risk factor that mandates radiography? Age ≥ 65, a dangerous mechanism, or paresthesias in the extremities → immediate x-ray. Dangerous mechanisms: a fall from ≥ 3 ft or 5 stairs; axial load to the head (e.g., diving); MVC at high speed (>100 km/h), rollover or ejection; motorized recreational vehicles; bicycle struck or collision.
  • 2. Any low-risk factor that allows safe assessment of ROM? A simple rear-end MVC, sitting in the ED, ambulatory at any time, delayed onset of neck pain, or no midline C-spine tenderness. If no low-risk factor is present (can't sit or walk, midline pain) → x-ray. A simple rear-end MVC excludes being pushed into oncoming traffic, hit by a bus or large truck, a rollover, or being hit by a high-speed vehicle. "Delayed" means the neck pain didn't start immediately.
  • 3. Able to actively rotate the neck 45° left and right? Able → no radiography. Unable → refer for x-ray. The clinician performs the C/S AROM.
  • Rule not applicable if: non-trauma cases, GCS < 15, unstable vital signs, age < 16, acute paralysis, known vertebral disease, previous C-spine surgery, or pregnancy.
  • Insufficiency fractures: consider them in osteoporotic populations, older individuals and people on long-term corticosteroids, even with relatively low loads.
2CLigamentous instability: causes, symptoms, which test for which ligament
Break it down

The gistThe upper cervical ligaments (transverse and alar) hold C1–2 together. They fail from trauma, from laxity you're born with (EDS, Down syndrome), or from erosion by inflammation (RA).

Watch forOnly test low-irritability patients. Sharp-Purser → transverse ligament. Alar ligament test → alar ligament.

  • Causes: trauma (tearing of the upper cervical ligaments or a fracture of the dens). Congenital general hypermobility: in Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, upper cervical instability may be relatively common (52–66%) but severe instability is less common (3%); consider the Beighton score. Down syndrome: transverse ligament laxity affects 10–30%. Rheumatoid arthritis: chronic synovitis can erode the dens and the transverse ligament insertion sites. 5
  • Symptoms: headaches; severe suboccipital or other muscle spasms; fear or anxiety with head motion; neurological symptoms that may be constant, transient or absent.
  • Testing (low irritability only): the Sharp-Purser test for the transverse ligament and the Alar ligament test for the alar ligament.
2DSharp-Purser, Alar ligament and Quadrant tests: how to do them and what's positive
Break it down

The gistSharp-Purser pushes the head back while C2 is fixed. Symptoms that ease or excess displacement during the A→P movement is positive. inference The mechanism (a lax transverse ligament letting C1 sit forward) is an interpretation, not a stated finding. The Alar test side-bends the head: C2's spinous process should move right away, because the alar ligament pulls it.

Watch forSharp-Purser is highly specific, +LR 17.25. The Alar test has poor reliability. The Quadrant test is for symptom provocation (facets or nerve compression), not for stability.

  • Sharp-Purser (transverse ligament, atlantoaxial stability). Patient seated with the neck semi-flexed; assess resting symptoms. Put your palm on the forehead and your index finger and thumb on the SP of the axis, then apply a posterior translation through the forehead. Positive: reproduction of myelopathic symptoms during forward flexion, a decrease in symptoms during the anterior-to-posterior movement, or excess displacement during the AP movement. If positive, refer out for radiograph and stabilization. Highly specific, +LR 17.25. 6 Note: C1 translates back; symptom change indicates a positive test.
  • Alar ligament test. Patient seated. Grasp the head with one hand and pincer-grasp the SP of C2 with the other. Passively side-bend the head right and feel for the transverse process to come into your fingers; assess end feel. You should feel immediate movement of the C2 SP with a ligamentous end feel. Return to neutral and repeat to the left. Positive: significant side bending with an empty end feel, and the transverse process doesn't come into the fingers. Poor reliability. Note: positive = no C2 movement into the palpating hand.
  • Cervical quadrant test with overpressure (symptom provocation, possibly from the facets or nerve compression). Patient seated: rotate and side-bend to the same side, then look up into extension. Look for ROM and symptom reproduction. If there are no symptoms, block the scapula with one hand, put the other palm on the crown of the head, and apply a downward force. Notes: check all ROMs (extension, rotation, flexion) and stop if pain is provoked; overpressure can be used for extension and rotation while blocking the scapula.
2EVascular pathology: two mechanisms, risk factors, red flags
Break it down

The gistNeck arteries cause trouble in two ways. Disease (atherosclerosis, with the usual cardiovascular risk factors) and trauma (a dissection that tears the inner lining). Neck pain is a common symptom of either.

Watch forNeck pain plus facial or peri-orbital pain, especially with vascular risk factors or after recent trauma, is a flag for a focused vascular and cranial nerve screen.

  • Two sources: cervical arterial disease and trauma (vertebral artery dissection). The arteries in question are the carotid and vertebral arteries. 7
  • Cervical arterial disease: risk factors are similar to those for cardiovascular disease: hyperlipidemia, hypertension, history of smoking. PTs should routinely measure blood pressure.
  • Vertebral artery dissection (VAD): cervical rotation combined with side flexion and extension can tear the intima of the vertebral artery. Blood pools in the arterial wall, clots form, and a clot can dislodge and cause a stroke.
  • Pain pattern: neck pain is a common symptom of vascular pathologies. When it comes with facial or peri-orbital pain, especially with vascular risk factors or after recent neck trauma, treat it as a flag for upper-cervical or vascular involvement and do a more focused vascular and cranial nerve screen. 8
  • IFOMPT patient history: dissecting stroke risk factors are recent trauma, a vascular anomaly, and current or past smoking. Non-dissecting stroke risk factors (cardiovascular, more common in older patients) are current or past smoking, hypertension and high cholesterol. 7
  • Most frequent signs (Thomas 2015 tables): VBA dissection leads with unsteadiness and ataxia (67%). ICA dissection leads with ptosis (60–80%).
2F5 D's and 3 N's, referral decisions, and why VBI testing is out
Break it down

The gistThe 5 D's and 3 N's are the cranial nerve and brainstem symptoms to ask about. If the picture is concerning, don't do manual therapy. Where you send the patient depends on how acute it is.

Watch forAcute (a thunderclap headache or new neuro signs) → emergency department. Subacute concern → the patient's physician. Positional VBI testing is no longer recommended. Note: it is still seen in clinical practice.

  • 5 D's: Drop attacks (sudden falls without loss of consciousness) · Dizziness related to neck movement · Dysphagia (difficulty swallowing) · Dysarthria (slow, slurred speech) · Diplopia (double vision). 3 N's: Nausea · Nystagmus · Numbness (facial). 7
  • When the picture raises concern for VBI, cervical artery dissection or other vascular pathology, defer manual therapy. Refer to the ED for acute presentations: a "thunderclap" headache (sudden, extremely severe head pain that peaks in ≤ 60 seconds) or new-onset neurological signs. Refer to the patient's physician for subacute concerns. 7, 9
  • BE FAST stroke warning signs: Balance, Eyes, Face, Arms, Speech, Time (call 911).
  • VBI testing: per the 2020 IFOMPT framework, "clearing" the C/S before manual therapy with VBI testing or end-range holds is no longer recommended. Positional testing has poor validity and shouldn't give false confidence when risk factors are present. Positive findings during positional testing or pre-manipulative holds can cause real neurological harm. VBI testing is not recommended when the history suggests vascular pathology. Note: still seen in clinical practice.
2GMalignancy and cervical myelopathy: presentation and the myelopathy cluster
Break it down

The gistMyelopathy is cord compression, so the signs are upper motor neuron signs (Hoffman's, Babinski, inverted supinator) plus gait change. Three of the five cluster findings strongly rule it in.

Watch for≥ 3 of 5 positive → +LR 30.9 and a post-test probability of 94% → immediate medical referral for advanced imaging. Myelopathy is often made worse by extension.

  • Malignancy: head and neck pain, sore throat, difficulty swallowing, ringing in the ears. Most common in men over 50. Risk factors: tobacco use, heavy alcohol use, HPV infection, chronic exposure to environmental carcinogens, Epstein-Barr virus, and prior radiation to the head and neck. 10
  • Myelopathy: often exacerbated with extension-based activities. Clinical prediction rule of 5 signs: (1) gait deviation, (2) positive Hoffman's, (3) positive inverted supinator sign, (4) positive Babinski, (5) age > 45. 3 or more positive → +LR 30.9, raising the post-test probability to 94%, which warrants immediate medical referral for advanced imaging. 11
  • Related: in a radiculopathy neuro screen, a 3+ reflex means you check for UMN pathology (Hoffman's, Babinski) to see whether it's a 4+. 12

Topic 3 · Neck pain classification (CPG)

CPG categories · headache types · radiculopathy · whiplash prognosis and treatment
3AThe 4 neck pain classifications, and why patients often fit more than one
Break it down

The gistThe CPG labels are impairment-based diagnoses. Real patients often have pieces of several. A movement diagnosis (like extension-rotation syndrome) can sit on top of the CPG label and individualize treatment.

Watch forOne patient can have radiating thumb pain, headaches and painful limited rotation all at once.

  • 1. Neck pain with mobility deficits · 2. Neck pain with headache · 3. Neck pain with radiating pain · 4. Neck pain with movement coordination impairments. 12
  • People often don't fit squarely into one category. Example: radiculopathy with lateral thumb pain (radiating), headaches (headache), and limited, painful C/S rotation with pain referring into one of Cloward's areas (mobility deficits). This patient is likely weak in the deep neck flexors, has poor postural habits, and may have multiple double-crush sites amplifying the thumb pain.
  • A movement-based diagnosis helps you choose interventions. Go back to the functional task and movement assessment to see whether a consistent movement pattern drives the symptoms. A movement diagnosis (e.g., cervical flexion or extension-rotation syndrome) can coexist with the neck pain classification.
3BNeck pain with mobility deficits: symptoms and CPG recommendations
Break it down

The gistThis is local neck pain with limited motion that consistently reproduces symptoms. Any referred pain should be reproducible by provoking the involved segment with joint play.

Watch forTreatment changes with phase. Acute: thoracic manipulation, neck ROM exercises, scapulothoracic and UE stretching and strengthening. Subacute: neck and shoulder girdle endurance. Chronic: multimodal. All are grade B.

  • Symptoms: central and/or unilateral neck pain; limited neck motion that consistently reproduces symptoms; restricted cervical (and potentially T/S) ROM and segmental mobility. Referred shoulder girdle or UE pain may be present. It should be reproduced by provoking the involved segment with joint play (unilateral PA, upglide, downglide). Know the C/S facet referral pattern (Dwyer figure: C2–3 through C6–7). 12, 13
  • Acute (B): thoracic manipulation, a program of neck ROM exercises, and scapulothoracic and UE stretching and strengthening to enhance program adherence.
  • Subacute (B): neck and shoulder girdle endurance exercises.
  • Chronic (B): a multimodal approach: thoracic manipulation and cervical manipulation or mobilization; mixed exercise for the cervical and scapulothoracic regions (neuromuscular exercise such as coordination, proprioception and postural training, plus stretching, strengthening, endurance, aerobic conditioning and cognitive-affective elements); and dry needling, laser or intermittent traction.

Watch forTension-type can look like cervicogenic, with trigger point tenderness, but the trigger points don't reproduce the primary headache. POUND = migraine.

3DCervicogenic headache: provocation, referral to the head, CPG recommendations
Break it down

The gistUpper cervical sensory fibers and the trigeminal nerve's descending tract meet in the trigeminocervical nucleus, which is why an upper neck problem can hurt in the head and face.

Watch forTo call it cervicogenic, the symptoms must be reproducible with provocation testing of O–C1, C1–2, C2–3 or the suboccipitals.

  • Tends to be unilateral and related to movement of the craniocervical spine and/or TMJ. Must be reproducible with provocation testing at O–C1, C1–2, C2–3, suboccipitals. 10
  • Trigeminocervical nucleus: the region of the upper C/S where sensory fibers of the descending trigeminal tract interact with fibers of the upper cervical roots.
  • What refers to the head and face: C0–1, C1–2, C2–3; the greater and lesser occipital nerves (with numbness); the muscles of mastication; C3-innervated muscles (upper trap, SCM, scalenes); C1-innervated muscles (suboccipitals).
  • CPG (all grade B): acute → supervised instruction in active mobility exercise. Subacute → cervical manipulation and mobilization. Chronic → cervical or cervicothoracic manipulation or mobilization combined with shoulder girdle and neck stretching, strengthening and endurance exercise. 12
3ECervical radiculopathy: signs, the rule-in cluster, neuro screen
Break it down

The gistSymptoms are worse with extension plus ipsilateral side bending and rotation, and early treatment aims to maximize the foramen ("opening", traction). inference That those motions narrow the foramen is an interpretation linking the aggravating motions to the opening goal.

Watch forThe four-item cluster: + distraction, ipsilateral rotation < 60°, + Spurling's, + ULTT A (median bias). C5–6 is most common (lateral arm). TOS is C8–T1 (medial arm).

  • Pain or symptoms (numbness, tingling) radiate to the distal UE or the medial scapular border (Cloward sign). Most often affects the C5–6 nerve roots (lateral arm). Worse with cervical extension and ipsilateral side bending and rotation. 12
  • Most likely with this cluster: + neck distraction test · ipsilateral C/S rotation AROM < 60° · + Spurling's test · + upper limb tension test A (median nerve bias).
  • Do a thorough neuro screen (segmental myotomes, dermatomes, reflexes). If a finding is positive, re-assess weekly. If reflexes are 3+, check for UMN pathology (Hoffman's, Babinski) to see if it's a 4+.
3FRadiculopathy treatment by phase; double crush vs TOS
Break it down

The gistEarly on, the nerve root is inflamed (the chemical phase), so open the foramen and leave the nerve alone. Later (the mechanical phase), restore joint motion, load the tissues, and use gentle nerve sliders.

Watch forAcute: avoid aggressive ULTT and nerve glides and avoid extension/ipsilateral SB-rotation. Chronic: strengthening the levator scap and upper trap is essential to offload compressive shear.

  • Acute, the chemical phase (typically the first 4–6 weeks): symptoms come from the nerve root's inflammatory response. The goal is to maximize the size of the IV foramen and minimize tissue irritation. Encourage "opening": mechanical traction, and avoid cervical extension and ipsilateral side bending or rotation. Avoid aggressive neurodynamic testing (ULTT) or treatment (nerve glides). 10
  • Chronic, the mechanical phase (beyond 12 weeks): graded manual therapy for joint mobility restrictions, progressing to extension and ipsilateral rotation while you monitor peripheral symptoms. Muscle conditioning and postural strengthening: strengthening the levator scap and upper trap is essential to dynamically offload compressive shear at the involved segment. Neural sliders/gliders remove intraneural edema and oxygenate hypoxic tissue.
  • Double crush: consecutive focal lesions along a nerve have an additive effect. It's common to have nerve root compression plus other entrapment sites, so treat all sites to unload the whole neural system. Multiple roots can also be compressed.
  • Thoracic outlet syndrome: most often affects C8–T1 (medial arm); use the + TOS clinical prediction rule.
3GMovement coordination impairments (whiplash): assessment and prognosis
Break it down

The gistThis is trauma or whiplash onset plus a sensorimotor problem: weak or poorly timed deep neck flexors, poor proprioception, and often concussion-like and affective symptoms.

Watch forPrognosis (Ritchie 2015): NDI ≤ 32% and young → full recovery. NDI ≥ 40%, age ≥ 35 and hyperarousal ≥ 6 → ongoing moderate/severe disability at 12 months.

  • Assessment: mechanism linked to trauma or whiplash; referred shoulder girdle or UE pain; varied nonspecific concussive signs (dizziness or nausea; headache, concentration or memory difficulties; confusion; hypersensitivity to mechanical, thermal, acoustic, odor or light stimuli); heightened affective distress (moods, feelings, attitudes); strength and endurance deficits of the neck muscles (weak deep neck flexors, + cranial cervical flexion test); sensorimotor impairment (altered muscle activation, proprioceptive deficit, postural balance or control). Assess DNF strength in patients with nonspecific concussive signs even without a traumatic cause. 12
  • Prognosis (Ritchie 2015): moderate/severe disability at 12 months is predicted with age ≥ 35, NDI ≥ 40%, and hyperarousal ≥ 6 on the Posttraumatic Diagnostic Scale subscale. Full recovery is predicted with younger age and NDI ≤ 32%. NDI 33–39% → neither prediction. note The text of the rule says "<35 years" for full recovery, while its flowchart branch reads "≤35 y".
3HMovement coordination impairment treatment by prognosis and phase
Break it down

The gistGood prognosis: keep it light and keep them moving. Poor prognosis: add manual therapy and stabilization. If it goes chronic, treat the sensitized system, not a tissue.

Watch forChronic phase is 3–6 months: nociplastic pain, so use graded exposure, aerobic work, PNE, CBT, vestibular rehab and joint position error training.

  • Positive prognosis, acute phase: a "light touch" approach: wean from the cervical collar, C/S AROM, postural awareness, stay active and return to normal activity. 12
  • Poorer prognosis, subacute: manual therapy for thoracic and cervical mobility, plus stabilization exercises.
  • Poorer prognosis, chronic (3–6 months): a nociplastic pain state, so focus on desensitizing the system rather than fixing a specific tissue. Use graded exposure to movement and aerobic conditioning, pain neuroscience education, CBT, vestibular rehabilitation, and cervical joint position error (eye-head-neck coordination) training. Gains may be limited and recovery prolonged.

Topic 4 · Movement System Impairment syndromes

Movement system impairment model · four cervical syndromes · posture and flexibility examination
4AWhy a movement system diagnosis: framework and rationale
Break it down

The gistPTs diagnose the movement system. The neck moves a lot, and repeated postures and movements change alignment and joint loading. That leads to degeneration and pain, so you treat the faulty movement, not just the pain.

Watch forThe 4 MSI syndromes are often grouped under "neck pain with movement coordination deficits", and a movement diagnosis can coexist with any CPG category.

  • How we diagnose: a tissue diagnosis can be limited. Impairment diagnoses are CPG classification categories linked to the ICF (mobility deficits, headache, radiating, movement coordination deficits). A movement diagnosis can also fall under movement coordination impairments. 14
  • Human movement system: it's the physical therapy identity. Diagnoses made by PTs are of the movement system: Movement System Impairment (MSI) syndromes.
  • The 4 syndromes: (1) cervical extension, (2) cervical extension-rotation, (3) cervical flexion, (4) cervical flexion-rotation.
  • Framework & rationale: the cervical spine is highly mobile, so it's prone to degeneration and faulty movement. Repeated postures and movements alter alignment and joint loading. The goal is to recognize and treat impairments through precise alignment and control.
  • Summary: precise control matters more than gross motion; correct impairments to reduce degenerative stress; treat the movement impairment, not just the pain.
4BCervical extension syndrome: definition, features, contributing factors
Break it down

The gistWhen the patient extends, the head slides (translates) instead of rotating cleanly in the sagittal plane. It usually rides along with forward head, thoracic kyphosis and a poorly positioned scapula.

Watch forYounger patients are flexible and may have too much extension or posterior translation. Older patients are stiff and have limited ROM from degeneration.

  • Definition: imprecise extension → excessive translation vs sagittal rotation. Common with forward head, thoracic kyphosis and scapular malalignment. 14
  • Clinical features: pain with extension or prolonged forward-head posture; excessive thoracic kyphosis; headaches (suboccipital region); radicular symptoms; facet > medial border of the scapula; limited, or excessive and painful, extension or excessive anterior translation.
  • Younger vs older: younger spines are flexible and may have excess extension ROM or posterior translation. Older spines are stiff, with degeneration and limited ROM. Forward head posture prevents ideal extension kinematics.
  • Contributing factors: forward head; lack of cervical segmental flexion and extension; overuse of the levator scapulae → shear on the cervical vertebrae; excessive thoracic kyphosis; poor scapular alignment.
  • Passive support test: passively elevating the shoulder girdle while the patient actively flexes or extends may eliminate symptoms and increase ROM. Or passively elevate the ribcage in patients with overdeveloped abdominals.
4CThe levator scapulae story: why it feels "tight" and what to do
Break it down

The gistA depressed scapula pulls down on the levator, producing sustained loading at C1–C4. The levator works isometrically to hold the load, so it's overloaded while lengthened and shears the upper neck.

Watch forAvoid constantly cueing "shoulders down and back", which can depress the scapula further. The goal is load capacity, not avoiding the levator.

  • The muscle can feel "tight" because it is overloaded while lengthened, not necessarily because it's anatomically short. 14
  • Heavy suitcase → downward pull on the scapula → isometric levator activity → sustained loading at C1–C4.
  • Avoid constantly forcing the shoulders "down and back", which may further depress the scapula. Unload the arms and bring items closer.
  • Gradually strengthen the levator and surrounding muscles when appropriate. The goal is to improve its load capacity and share the work, not to avoid using it.
4DCervical extension syndrome: intervention principles
Break it down

The gistThere are 3 principles: fix alignment, strengthen the deep stabilizers (long extensors and intrinsic flexors), and turn down the big superficial muscles (levator, SCM).

Watch forForward head means cervical flexion is poorly distributed: too much lower-cervical flexion and not enough upper-cervical flexion. Correct segmental flexion, and retrain extension in quadruped.

  • 1. Restore neutral cervical, thoracic and scapular alignment and segmental rotation. 2. Strengthen the long cervical extensors and the intrinsic cervical flexors. 3. Reduce extrinsic dominance (levator scapulae, SCM). 14
  • Forward-head posture is associated with poorly distributed cervical flexion: excessive lower-cervical flexion and insufficient upper-cervical flexion. Forward head causes a lack of segmental flexion, so correct segmental flexion.
  • Correct extension in quadruped (see the deep neck extensor coordination test in 5H for the cues).
4ECervical extension-rotation syndrome
Break it down

The gistThis is the most common cervical syndrome. When the patient rotates, the neck also extends or side-bends instead of turning cleanly about a vertical axis, and usually one side's muscles and scapula are pulling asymmetrically.

Watch forThe three intervention verbs: Train precise rotation about a vertical axis · Support the arms to unload the C-spine · Strengthen the intrinsic rotators and deep neck flexors.

  • Definition: the most common cervical syndrome; imprecise rotation with compensatory extension or side bending. 14
  • Clinical features: unilateral pain, often linked to vision or hearing deficits; limited or painful rotation with extension or lateral flexion; asymmetry in musculature and scapular alignment.
  • Contributing factors: habitual head postures; UE movements that stress the cervicoscapular link; asymmetrical neck muscles or scapular positioning.
  • Intervention: Train precise rotation about a vertical axis. Support the arms to unload the cervical spine. Strengthen the intrinsic rotators and deep neck flexors. Minimize asymmetrical SCM/scalene dominance, and balance flexibility and strength of the shoulder muscles.
  • Identify an asymmetrical pull of the levator and traps: correct it by having the patient bring the arms up together while stabilizing with the deep neck flexors, or lift the arms and rotate the head.
4FCervical flexion syndrome
Break it down

The gistThis is the flat-backed person who hurts with sitting and reading. The lower neck does too much of the flexion, the thoracic spine doesn't help, and the big flexors (SCM, scalenes) overpower the deep ones.

Watch forIntervention: Strengthen the long spine extensors · Normalize flexion distribution (have the patient slump) · Correct thoracic alignment.

  • Definition: decreased cervical lordosis and a flat thoracic spine; excessive flexion of the lower cervical vs the upper thoracic spine. 14
  • Clinical features: pain with flexion tasks (sitting, reading); dominance of the extrinsic flexors (SCM, scalenes); weak intrinsic flexors.
  • Contributing factors: depressed or downwardly rotated scapula; poor thoracic mobility; muscle imbalance (deep flexor weakness, extensor underuse).
  • Intervention: Strengthen the long spine extensors; normalize flexion distribution (have the patient slump); correct thoracic alignment.
4GCervical flexion-rotation syndrome
Break it down

The gistThis is the flexion syndrome with a twist: rotation happens together with flexion, and the thoracic spine doesn't flex its share.

Watch forThe intervention order is 1 correct thoracic and scapular alignment to neutral → 2 restore thoracic flexion → 3 restore moving the C-spine on its axis.

  • Definition: flat thoracic spine with decreased lordosis; imprecise rotation with concurrent flexion. 14
  • Clinical features: pain with flexion plus rotation; lack of thoracic flexion; poor distribution across cervical segments.
  • Contributing factors: habitual asymmetric postures; scapular malalignment; excessive recruitment of the extrinsic rotators (levator vs obliquus capitis inferior; obliquus capitis inferior and splenius).
  • Intervention: (1) correct thoracic and scapular alignment to a neutral posture; (2) restore thoracic flexion; (3) restore moving the C-spine on its axis.

Break it down

The gistA low, anteriorly tilted scapula means the scapulocervical muscles hang off her neck. Right arm elevation pulled her C-spine into left rotation, and unloading the shoulders reduced her pain. The diagnosis was extension-rotation.

Watch forHer treatment is mostly unloading: armrests, arms propped up, hair down, a slant board and phone position, plus self-mobilization.

5BCervical PROM with overpressure and PAIVM/joint play: what each tells you
Break it down

The gistPROM tells you how much motion there is, its quality, end feel and symptoms. Central and unilateral PAs tell you where the pain comes from and whether a level is hypo- or hypermobile, but not which direction is limited. That's what upglide and downglide testing is for.

Watch forNote: hypo above and below the hinge, hyper at the hinge.

  • PROM (supine, both hands holding the occiput): gently take the neck into full flexion, extension, rotation both ways and side bend both ways, returning to neutral each time. 5CUnilateral PA: setup, cues, and choosing the grade
Break it down

The gistGrade by the pain-resistance relationship. Pain before resistance means an irritable joint, so use a gentle grade I–II. Resistance with pain, or resistance dominant, means a stiff joint, so use grade III–IV.

Watch forUse it when the pain is unilateral. Apply a 30° medial angle so you don't slip off the pillar, and don't induce side bending or rotation.

  • Use when pain is unilateral. Move laterally until you hit the facet joint. Purpose: assess and improve a segmental mobility deficit. ICF: mobility deficits, radiating pain, headache. 6
  • Position: patient prone with a pillow under the ankles, head and neck in neutral rotation, plinth head angled slightly down. Therapist at the head of the table, facing the patient's feet.
  • Cues: elbows extended; both thumbs apply a posterior-to-anterior force to each articular pillar. Sink in and assess mobility, pain or spasm. Use your other fingers to pull the lateral musculature posteriorly to create slack. Apply PA pressure at a 30° medial angle to avoid slipping off the pillar. Check for symptom reproduction and the pain-resistance relationship.
  • Grade: pain before resistance → Grade I or II. Resistance and pain together, or resistance dominant → Grade III or IV. Be gentle; don't induce side bending or rotation. Note: higher irritability → lower grade.
  • As treatment: you're not mobilizing to gain motion in a particular direction. You're mobilizing because joint play testing showed hypomobility. Retest the functional activity or aggravating factor to see whether it worked.

5EUpglide and downglide techniques: hand placement and direction
Break it down

The gistUpglide: push the cranial segment's pillar up and forward at 45° toward the opposite eye, with the segment below stabilized. Downglide: lock in at the cranial pillar and press at 45° toward the opposite elbow or shoulder, then use the other hand to side-bend and rotate toward the mobilizing hand until you find a crisp end feel.

Watch forDownglide has no stabilization and you don't lift the head; the head is off the end of the table. For upglide, some clinicians prefer not to lift the head.

  • Upglide (assess and increase mobility; ICF: mobility deficit, radiating pain). Patient supine; therapist standing at the head of the table. Gently lift the head and stabilize it with your abdomen (or leave the head resting, which some clinicians prefer). Reinforce the 2nd digit with the 3rd on the mobilizing hand and place them on the articular pillar of the cranial segment. The non-mobilizing hand either acts as a fulcrum on the contralateral lateral mass of the caudal segment, or stabilizes the segment below by hooking the contralateral SP. Direction: 45° toward the opposite eye. Move down the spine comparing level to level and side to side, looking for ROM or symptom reproduction. If the chin juts forward, place a thumb gently on the chin. If you feel a stiff segment, treat the same way. 6
  • Downglide (assess how the facets close; ICF: mobility deficit). Patient supine, relaxed, with the head off the end of the table; therapist at the head of the bed. No stabilization. Look for a "crisp" capsular end feel. Place the 1st MCP of the mobilizing hand on the articular pillar of the cranial segment, with the other hand cradling the occiput. Don't lift the head. "Lock in" the mobilizing hand by extending at the segment and pressing at 45° toward the patient's opposite elbow or shoulder, depending on the plane of resistance. Use the other hand to add traction, side bending and rotation toward the mobilizing hand while keeping it locked in, searching for the crisp end feel. Assess end feel and pain at each segment. Note: one hand can hold the head in a chin tuck if needed.
5FCervical SNAG (Mulligan): dosage, rules, cues
Break it down

The gistYou hold an upglide on the painful side while the patient actively turns away, and you keep holding it all the way back to neutral. Mulligan techniques must be pain free.

Watch forDosage is 1–2 sets of 6–10 reps. If it hurts, change your angle of force. Afterward, have the patient actively use the new range as a "save button".

  • Dosage: 1–2 sets of 6–10 reps. Mulligan techniques should be pain free; the goal is more ROM without pain. If the technique is painful, modify your angle of force. Purpose: decrease pain and improve segmental mobility (ICF: mobility deficit). 6
  • Setup: patient sitting in neutral spine on a chair or stool; therapist behind. Place your medial thumb on the segment on the side of pain, with your lateral thumb on top. The thumbs apply traction as an upglide: superior/anterior force, toward the eye.
  • Movement: the patient rotates to the opposite side (this can also be done with side bending) while you maintain the glide. The glide is held until the patient is back in neutral; rotate your body with the patient. Release and repeat.
  • After mobilizing to gain motion, have the patient actively move through the new ROM as a "save button", especially rotation toward the side of limitation.
5GUpper thoracic techniques: central PA, unilateral PA, reverse NAG, T1–T2 rotation SNAG
Break it down

The gistFor the upper T/S, AROM is the same as for the C/S, but you watch the upper T/S and the CT junction. The four techniques differ by position (prone vs sitting) and by what you push on (SP vs one side).

Watch forReverse NAG: a PA wedge on the T1 or T2 SP while you take the head into retraction. T1–T2 rotation SNAG: glide T1 and T2 in opposite rotational directions.

  • Central PA (prone, arms at sides, pillow under the feet; therapist at the side of the plinth): make a "dummy thumb" with one thumb pad over the other on the SP. Lock your elbows, lean your sternum over your hands, apply central PA pressure, and assess from R1 to R2. Repeat at a therapeutic graded force, and reduce or stop according to symptoms. Treatment: you can use the pisiform on the SP and pre-position the head in flexion or extension, depending on the direction you want to improve. 6
  • Unilateral PA (prone; therapist at the head of the plinth): move about 1 inch lateral from the T1 SP toward the uninvolved side, make a dummy thumb over the target, lock your elbows with your sternum over your hands, and apply PA pressure assessing hypo- or hypermobility. You can grade it for mobility or pain, and pre-position the head (flexion, extension or rotation).
  • Reverse NAG (sitting on the edge of the plinth; therapist behind): assess resting symptoms. Wedge your thumb and index finger over the involved T1 or T2 SP and apply PA pressure, wrap your other arm around the head and move it into retraction, then release and repeat.
  • T1–T2 rotation SNAG (sitting): assess resting symptoms, then AROM rotation of the head and upper thoracic spine. If it hurts going right, put one thumb on the right lateral surface of the T1 SP and the other on the left lateral surface of the T2 SP, and apply a rotational glide in opposite directions. This can also be done at C7–T1.
5HDeep neck extensor coordination test (quadruped): cues and fail criteria
Break it down

The gistIn quadruped, "lift your head" usually becomes a chin poke (upper cervical extension). The correction keeps the eyes on a spot between the hands and lifts through the upper back, so the upper neck stays neutral.

Watch forFail = excessive upper cervical extension, can't reach full head lift in upper cervical neutral, or can't do 5–10 smooth, controlled reps.

  • Purpose: assess deep neck extensor strength, endurance and coordination. ICF: movement coordination impairments, mobility deficits. Patient in quadruped; therapist to the side. 6, 16
  • Ask them to "Lift up your head." Watch for faulty patterns: initiating with craniocervical extension, excessive extension at one area with significant creasing of the skin, and symptom reproduction.
  • Correct: "Lift your head while keeping your eyes on a spot (like a book or phone) right between your hands. Don't let your eyes leave this spot." Cue "Lift through your upper back" while manually cueing at the cervicothoracic junction. This keeps the upper cervical spine neutral. Reassess and ask whether symptoms changed, then repeat 5–10 times.
  • Fails if: they lift the head with excessive upper cervical extension; they can't reach the full head lift in upper cervical neutral; or they can't complete 5–10 smooth, controlled reps.
  • As treatment: more reps, isometric holds or resistance bands improve coordination, strength and endurance.
5IHeadache-focused upper cervical exam: where to look and what to prioritize

  1. Facet mechanics → AROM → joint play → treatmentRotation opens the contralateral facet and closes the ipsilateral one (Topic 1).   2. The hinge point shows up three timesPosture examination looks for a hinge point.   3. 60° of rotation, three different contextsIpsilateral rotation < 60° is one item in the radiculopathy cluster (Topic 3 and 1C).   5. The levator scapulae: a problem and a solutionOverused levator causes shear in extension syndrome, and a depressed scapula keeps it loaded at C1–C4. Case 01

Rear-ended at a stoplight

T2

A 42-year-old was rear-ended at low speed two hours ago. She is alert and stable, walked into the ED, and says her neck pain started about an hour after the crash. She has no midline tenderness and no paresthesias.

  1. Walk through the Canadian C-Spine Rule.
  2. What decides whether she needs an x-ray?
Model answer
  • Applicable: alert, medically stable, acute trauma, age ≥ 16, and none of the exclusions.
  • Step 1, high risk: age < 65, simple rear-end MVC (not a dangerous mechanism), no paresthesias, so no mandatory x-ray.

Case 06

One-sided headache after studying

T3T1

A 24-year-old grad student has right-sided, dull suboccipital headaches that spread over the eye after long study sessions. Extension and right rotation are limited, and pressing on C1–2 on the right reproduces her headache.

Case 07

The tennis player who turns and hurts

T4

A 30-year-old right-handed tennis player has left neck pain when she turns to the left to track the ball. As she turns, her head tips back. Her right scapula sits lower than her left.

  1. Movement system diagnosis?
  2. Three intervention principles?
Model answer
  • Cervical extension-rotation syndrome: imprecise rotation with compensatory extension, unilateral pain, asymmetric scapular alignment, and UE movements stressing the cervicoscapular link. It's the most common cervical syndrome.

01Describe the arthrokinematics of C2–7 during right rotation. Which side upglides and which downglides?
At C2–7, rotation produces opening/upglide on the contralateral side (the left facets glide superior and anterior) and closing/downglide on the ipsilateral side (the right facets glide inferior and posterior). C2–7 contributes about 30–35° of combined rotation; C1–2 contributes 55% of total rotation (35–40°). 1
02List the steps of the Canadian C-Spine Rule and name 3 situations where it doesn't apply.
  • High-risk factor (age ≥ 65, dangerous mechanism, paresthesias in the extremities) → radiography.
  • Low-risk factor that allows safe ROM assessment (simple rear-end MVC, sitting in the ED, ambulatory at any time, delayed onset of neck pain, no midline tenderness). None present → radiography.
  • Able to actively rotate 45° left and right? Yes → no radiography. No → radiography.
  • Not applicable: non-trauma, GCS < 15, unstable vitals, age < 16, acute paralysis, known vertebral disease, previous C-spine surgery, pregnancy.

(1) Dizziness, blackouts or drop attacks? (2) History of RA or inflammatory arthritis treated with systemic steroids? (3) Neurological symptoms in the legs? Ask them with all cervical patients, as part of screening for serious conditions (vascular pathology, ligamentous instability, myelopathy).

06What is the myelopathy clinical prediction rule, and what does a positive result mean?
Five findings: gait deviation, + Hoffman's, + inverted supinator sign, + Babinski, age > 45. ≥ 3 positive → +LR 30.9, post-test probability 94% → immediate medical referral for advanced imaging. Myelopathy is often exacerbated by extension-based activities. 11
07Name the four neck pain classifications and one hallmark of each.
  • Mobility deficits: limited motion that consistently reproduces symptoms.
  • Headache: cervicogenic; unilateral, reduced AROM, reproducible with upper cervical provocation.
  • Radiating pain: symptoms to the distal UE or medial scapular border; the radiculopathy cluster.
  • Movement coordination impairments: trauma/whiplash onset, concussive signs, weak deep neck flexors.
12
08What cluster rules in cervical radiculopathy, and which nerve root is most often involved?
+ neck distraction test, ipsilateral rotation AROM < 60°, + Spurling's, + ULTT A (median nerve bias). Most often C5–6 (lateral arm). Symptoms are worse with extension and ipsilateral SB and rotation; Cloward sign is pain at the medial scapular border. 12
09Compare treatment in the chemical vs mechanical phase of radiculopathy.
Chemical (first 4–6 weeks): nerve root inflammation; maximize the IV foramen and minimize irritation. Encourage "opening" with mechanical traction; avoid extension and ipsilateral SB/rotation; avoid aggressive ULTT or nerve glides. Mechanical (> 12 weeks): graded manual therapy progressing to extension and ipsilateral rotation while monitoring peripheral symptoms; conditioning and postural strengthening (levator scap and upper trap to offload shear); neural sliders/gliders. 10
10What predicts poor vs full recovery after whiplash (Ritchie 2015)?
Ongoing moderate/severe disability: age ≥ 35, NDI ≥ 40%, and hyperarousal ≥ 6 on the PDS hyperarousal subscale. Full recovery: younger age (< 35) and NDI ≤ 32%. NDI 33–39% predicts neither. 19
11Define cervical extension syndrome and give its 3 intervention principles.
Imprecise extension with excessive translation vs sagittal rotation; common with forward head, thoracic kyphosis and scapular malalignment. Interventions: (1) restore neutral cervical, thoracic and scapular alignment and segmental rotation; (2) strengthen the long cervical extensors and intrinsic cervical flexors; (3) reduce extrinsic dominance (levator scapulae, SCM). 14
12Why might the levator scapulae feel "tight" in extension syndrome, and how do you address it?
A depressed scapula (heavy suitcase, shoulders forced down) pulls the levator into sustained isometric work, loading C1–C4. It feels tight because it's overloaded while lengthened, not anatomically short. Stop constantly forcing the shoulders "down and back", unload the arms and bring items closer, and gradually strengthen the levator to improve load capacity and share the work. 14
13Describe extension-rotation syndrome: definition, features, and treatment.
The most common cervical syndrome: imprecise rotation with compensatory extension or side bending. Features: unilateral pain (often linked to vision or hearing deficits), limited or painful rotation with extension or lateral flexion, asymmetric muscles and scapular alignment. Treatment: train precise rotation about a vertical axis, support the arms to unload the C-spine, strengthen the intrinsic rotators and deep neck flexors, and minimize SCM/scalene asymmetrical dominance. 14
14How do AROM findings differ between an upglide and a downglide restriction?
Upglide/opening restriction: limited contralateral rotation and SF; deviation into extension with contralateral rotation/SF; deviation toward the restricted side with flexion; usually above the hinge (C2–4). Downglide/closing restriction: limited ipsilateral rotation and SF; deviation into flexion with ipsilateral rotation/SF; deviation away from the restricted side with extension; usually the lower C/S (C6–7). 6
15How do you choose the grade for a unilateral PA, and how do you perform a cervical SNAG?
Grade: pain before resistance → Grade I–II; pain and resistance together, or resistance dominant → Grade III–IV. SNAG: patient sitting; thumbs on the segment on the painful side, applying an upglide (superior/anterior, toward the eye) while the patient rotates to the opposite side. Hold the glide back to neutral. It must be pain free; 1–2 sets of 6–10. Finish with active ROM as a "save button". 6
16Describe the deep neck extensor coordination test and its fail criteria.
Quadruped; "lift up your head." Watch for initiation with craniocervical extension, excessive extension at one area (skin creasing) and symptom reproduction. Correct with eyes on a spot between the hands and "lift through your upper back" with a manual cue at the CT junction, keeping the upper C/S neutral. Repeat 5–10 times. Fails if: excessive upper cervical extension, can't reach full head lift in upper cervical neutral, or can't complete 5–10 smooth, controlled reps. 6
17Describe the flexion-rotation test: setup, a positive finding and the norms. Name two treatments if it's positive.
Patient supine, scooted up the table. Passively flex the lower cervical spine to maximal flexion, stabilize with your abdomen, hold the head on both sides and rotate it each way while keeping full flexion; compare range, symmetry and symptoms. Positive: symptom reproduction or loss of range > 10°. Normal is about 44° each way; subjects with headache from C1–2 dysfunction average 25° or less. Treatments for limited C1–2 rotation: C1–2 contract-relax, supine C1–2 rotation mobilization (Kaltenborn) or a self C1–2 SNAG, followed by active rotation as a "save button". 6, 17, 18

Points the cited material doesn't settle, plus places where sources disagree.

Normal cervical ROM varies by reference

Expected AROM on observation: flexion 45–50°, extension 50–80°, side bend 45–50°, rotation ≈80°. Goniometric norms: 0–45° for flexion, extension and lateral flexion, and 0–60° for rotation. State which reference you use.

Special tests named but not described

Spurling's, the neck distraction test, ULTT A, Hoffman's, inverted supinator, Babinski, the cranial cervical flexion test and the TOS clinical prediction rule are named as findings here, but their procedures aren't described in this guide.

Time cut-offs for acute, subacute and chronic neck pain

The CPG recommendations are split by phase, but the phases aren't defined in weeks here. The only timing given is radiculopathy's chemical phase (first 4–6 weeks) and mechanical phase (> 12 weeks), and the MCI chronic phase (3–6 months).

Small inconsistencies between sources

Whiplash prognosis: the rule's text says full recovery at "<35 years", while the flowchart branch reads "≤35 y". C1–2 side flexion: some descriptions allow small amounts, while Neumann lists no motion. The IFOMPT framework is dated 2020 in some sources and cited as Rushton et al. 2023 in others.
